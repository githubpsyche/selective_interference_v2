Participants view distressing film material, record later intrusive memories of the film, and often complete voluntary-memory tests for the same material.
This design makes it possible to ask whether the same encoded event can remain accessible to deliberate retrieval while becoming more or less likely to come to mind involuntarily.
In such research, intrusion frequency is often only weakly related to voluntary-memory performance, such that the two measures dissociate [@holmes2004trauma; @james2016trauma].
The same encoded episode can therefore support both involuntary and deliberate remembering, and these modes can come apart empirically.

Participants view a distressing film, record intrusions that later come to mind unintentionally, and often complete voluntary-memory tests covering its contents.
In such research, intrusion frequency is often only weakly related to voluntary-memory performance [@holmes2004trauma; @james2016trauma].
Intervention studies provide a stronger test of whether these forms of access can be affected differently.

Researchers have examined this dissociation using imagery-competing procedures, which ask participants to perform a brief, demanding visuospatial activity, such as complex pattern tapping or mental rotation during game play, either soon after film viewing or after a reminder has brought the film to mind [@deeprose2012imagery; @highfield2025summary; @holmes2010key].
In postencoding studies, these procedures can reduce later film intrusions, sometimes while voluntary memory for the same material remains relatively intact [@asselbergs2024effectiveness; @debettencourt2026ai; @deeprose2012imagery; @holmes2009can; @holmes2010key; @lau2019intrusive; @lau2021selectively].
Delayed-return variants present a film reminder before the task a day or more after film viewing and can also reduce subsequent intrusions [@james2015computer; @kessler2020visuospatial].
Related procedures have reduced intrusive memories after real-world trauma [@beckenstrom2026digital; @iyadurai2023reducing; @kanstrup2024guided; @kessler2018reducing; @ramineni2023treating], although these studies do not necessarily establish selective interference because not all include a voluntary-memory test.
Reduced intrusions accompanied by relatively preserved voluntary memory constitute the selective-interference pattern examined here [@lau2019intrusive; @lau2021selectively].

The selective-interference pattern has been used to argue that intrusive and voluntary remembering depend on separate representations of the same experience that differ in their vulnerability to interference [@brewin1996dual; @brewin2014episodic; @lau2019intrusive; @lau2021selectively].
Dual-representation accounts give this interpretation an architectural basis.
They link coherent deliberate recollection to hippocampally supported representations that bind event elements within a spatiotemporal context, and intrusive re-experiencing more closely to sensory and affective representations supported by amygdala and sensory systems [@bird2012hippocampus; @bisby2020reduced; @brewin2010intrusive].
In postencoding studies, imagery-competing tasks are proposed to disrupt sensory-perceptual imagery while the film memory remains active following film viewing [@baddeley2000working; @deeprose2012imagery; @highfield2025summary; @holmes2009can; @holmes2010key].
Delayed interventions instead use a reminder to reactivate the memory before the task, allowing later availability to be altered through reconsolidation or post-reactivation updating [@kindt2009beyond; @james2015computer].
Within a dual-representation interpretation, a larger reduction in intrusions across these variants is attributed to greater disruption of the sensory-bound representation that supports them than of the contextualized representation proposed to support deliberate recollection.

The force of this inference depends on what the compared measures require.
Intrusion reports, recall, and recognition differ not only in whether retrieval is intended, but also in whether film material must be brought to mind or is presented for judgment.
These differences leave open whether selective interference reflects separately vulnerable representations or competition and control within a common memory system.

# A Retrieved-Context Account of Selective Interference

Here we argue that preserved voluntary memory does not, by itself, establish that a separate representation was spared.
Selective interference can instead arise when task material becomes a competitor within a common memory system and retrieval goals and test format determine how strongly that competition affects performance.

Retrieved-context models offer a general theory of episodic memory search in which context can guide targeted recall and give rise to unintended intrusions [@cohen2022memory; @lohnas2015expanding; @polyn2011semantic].
Yet, to our knowledge, they have not been used to explain why post-reminder task interference can affect unguided and deliberate retrieval of the same material differently.
We formalize this account in an emotional variant [@cohen2022memory; @talmi2019retrieved] of the Context Maintenance and Retrieval (CMR) model [@polyn2009context].
The model generates unguided and deliberate recall from the same learned associations while allowing deliberate retrieval to control how those associations are used.
Preserved voluntary memory thereby becomes an outcome for a retrieved-context model to explain rather than an assumption attached to deliberate retrieval.
