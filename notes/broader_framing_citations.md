Yes. The question is not invented from nothing, but I don’t think there is one established literature that already owns it in this exact form. The closest pre-existing work is split across several literatures.

**Best Direct Precedents**

1. **Selective memory retrieval: external cues plus internal goals**

This is probably the closest match to the “how does goal-directed remembering stay selective?” half of the question.

Moccia/Morcom-style work asks how current goals and external retrieval cues jointly determine whether retrieval selects target memories over non-targets. The 2025 paper title is almost on point: **“The Effects of External Cue Overlap and Internal Goals on Selective Memory Retrieval...”** Its abstract says selection depends on both external cues and goal-directed control, and that target selectivity depends on cue overlap with targets versus non-targets.

Use for: the idea that selectivity is not just intention; it depends on whether retrieval cues overlap more with the target than with competitors.

Sources: [Moccia et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40653318/), [bioRxiv version](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.21.513221v3.full-text).

2. **Competitive retrieval reinstates competitor and target contexts**

Bramão et al. is a strong fit for the “context that cues target is bound to competitors” side. In an AB/AC interference paradigm, they found that proactive interference was accompanied by early reinstatement of the competitor context, and interference resolution by later target-context reinstatement.

Use for: the idea that competitive remembering involves reinstatement of contextual features of both target and competitor episodes.

Source: [Bramão et al., 2022](https://academic.oup.com/cercor/article/32/22/5020/6519540).

3. **Retrieved-context models of memory search**

CMR/TCM is the model family that gives us the machinery: current context cues candidate items; retrieved items reinstate associated context; semantic/source/task context can organize recall. Relevant sources already in your bib include Howard & Kahana, Sederberg et al., Polyn et al., and Lohnas et al.

Use for: the formal account of why context-bound competitors enter candidate sampling.

Local keys: `howard2002distributed`, `sederberg2008context`, `polyn2009context`, `polyn2009task`, `lohnas2015expanding`.

4. **Semantic/category-cued selective recall**

Polyn et al. 2011 and specialized-recall work are close to the film-goal idea. They show that people can use semantic/category structure to guide recall while temporal organization remains visible. Norman et al. 2017 adds neural evidence for sustained target/category states during selective recall.

Use for: deliberate recall remaining selective because a maintained target/category cue supports desired items without equally supporting competitors.

Local keys: `polyn2011semantic`, `lohnas2026specialized`, `norman2017neuronal`.

5. **Episodic updating and context-dependent interference**

Hupbach et al. and Sederberg et al. are relevant to the reminder-linked encoding side: returning to, or reinstating, a prior context can make later material become linked to the earlier episode. Sederberg et al. specifically show how a reconsolidation-like intrusion pattern can be explained with temporal context.

Use for: why a film reminder before task encoding can bind task material into film-associated context.

Local keys: `hupbach2008dynamics`, `sederberg2011human`.

6. **Retrieval-induced forgetting and context accounts**

RIF is adjacent, not central. It studies how selective retrieval changes later accessibility of related material. Jonker et al. argue for context change/reinstatement accounts of RIF, contrasting inhibition and association-strength accounts.

Use for: broader memory-control context, especially if discussing whether selective retrieval protects targets by suppressing competitors versus changing/reinstating context.

Sources: [Jonker et al., 2015](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963721415573203), [Anderson et al., 1994 reference list entry in Jonker](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963721415573203).

**Where Your Paper’s Niche Seems To Be**

The existing work covers the parts:

- context binds events and can create interference;
- competing contexts are reinstated during retrieval;
- goals and cue overlap can make retrieval selective;
- context models can formalize candidate competition;
- recognition/probe formats change access routes.

Your paper’s distinctive synthesis is:

> When a reminder makes new competitor material bind to target-associated context, goal-directed remembering remains selective only when retrieval has target-diagnostic support beyond that compromised context.

That is not just “dissociations are ambiguous.” It is a specific general-memory question about **selective retrieval under contaminated contextual cueing**.

## 

The closest line of work is **retrieval orientation / selective recollection** work, especially Moccia & Morcom.

The core question there is:

> When people are trying to retrieve only one kind of memory, what lets retrieval select targets without also reinstating non-targets?

That is very close to your question, except their targets/non-targets are usually **source categories** like pictures vs heard words, rather than film vs task material.

**The Basic Design**
In [Moccia & Morcom, 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13415-021-00971-0), participants studied objects in two formats: pictures or spoken object names. At test, one source was designated as the target. For example, accept items studied as pictures and reject items studied as spoken words, or vice versa.

They manipulated **cue overlap**:

- visual word test cues overlap more with the heard-word source;
- line-drawing cues overlap more with the picture source.

The important finding was that recollection became target-selective when the test cue overlapped more with the targeted source than the competing source. This was not just because one source was easier overall. Picture memory was generally better, but selectivity followed cue overlap, not simple memory strength.

**Key Concepts**

- **Retrieval goal:** “Retrieve picture-studied items” or “retrieve heard-word items.”
- **External cue overlap:** whether the test cue resembles target traces more than competitor traces.
- **Retrieval orientation:** a preparatory/control state that changes how cues are processed in line with the retrieval goal.
- **Selective recollection:** target memories are recollected more than non-target memories, rather than both being reinstated.

**Why This Matters For You**
This gives a very useful precedent for saying:

> Goal-directed remembering is selective only when the retrieval goal can be implemented through cues that discriminate targets from competitors.

Translated into your paper:

- Film goal = target retrieval orientation.
- Film items = target source.
- Task items encoded in film context = competitors.
- Film-related context = a cue that may no longer discriminate film from task.
- Film-specific semantic/sensory/source support = a more diagnostic retrieval basis.
- Recognition probe = an even more diagnostic cue because the item itself is presented.

So their work supports the idea that **intention alone is not enough**. A participant can intend to retrieve film memories, but selectivity depends on whether the cue/control state overlaps more with film memories than task competitors.

**2025 Extension**
The newer [Moccia et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40653318/) paper pushes this further using neural pattern reinstatement. Their title is basically your neighboring claim: **external cue overlap and internal goals jointly shape selective memory retrieval**. They report target-selective reinstatement when cues overlapped more with targets, reversed/non-target-biased reinstatement when cues overlapped more with non-targets, and some evidence for proactive goal-driven reinstatement before retrieval cues.

**Limitations For Our Use**
This is not a trauma-film or interference-intervention paper. It does not show reminder-linked task encoding. It also uses recognition/exclusion-style tasks, not unguided recall. So it should not carry your whole mechanism.

But it is highly relevant for the **retrieval-protection** part of your account:

> Deliberate remembering is protected when the retrieval goal can be implemented through cue dimensions that distinguish target material from competitors.

##

Both these Moccia papers both seem super relevant to our stuff. Their work is about how goals and cues support selective retrieval when they discriminate targets from competitors. 
 
Their 2022 ERP paper argues that selection can operate before recollection when retrieval cues overlap more with targeted than competing traces, and the 2025 paper extends this with neural reinstatement evidence that selection depends on external cues and goal-directed control. In general they show that goal-directed remembering depends on whether goals and available cues discriminate targets from competitors.
 
Our paper situates that idea in a context-binding account of interference and in the trauma film paradigm. Reminder-linked task encoding can bind task material to film-associated context, making film context a less selective cue. Nevertheless, deliberate recall can be protected when a maintained film-directed cue discriminates film material from task material despite that shared context.

Their work also suggests an account of why some reminder-linked task interventions are more amenable to lead to selective interference than others. In the trauma-film paradigm, the canonical intervention is a visuospatial task such as Tetris after a film reminder; similarly engaging comparison tasks, such as listening to a podcast, are not expected to create the same pattern of reduced intrusions with relatively preserved voluntary memory. The reason for the distinction suggested by the Moccia papers (and our model so far) is that it's easier to construct an item/semantic cue that distinguishes between film and visuospatial task material in memory vs other kinds of task material an experiment might use.
