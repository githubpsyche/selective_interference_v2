19                                          Abstract
20
21

22   The finding that a visuospatial intervention can decrease involuntary memories

23   following a laboratory analogue of trauma, without altering voluntary memory, is a

24   crucial source of support for the Dual Representation Account (DRA) of intrusive

25   memories. In two pre-registered experiments using Bayesian sequential designs, we

26   tested predictions derived from the DRA. In Experiment 1, we first aimed to replicate

27   the functional dissociation between voluntary and involuntary memory after a

28   visuospatial interference intervention, while matching retrieval conditions more closely

29   than in previous research. Second, we tested the prediction that the effect of the

30   intervention would be greater in an analogue trauma condition than a neutral control

31   condition. Third, we tested the prediction that voluntary memory would be decreased

32   in the trauma condition relative to the neutral condition. Finally, we hypothesized that

33   measures of voluntary and involuntary memory would not be correlated across

34   participants. Unexpectedly, the results indicated that the visuospatial intervention did

35   not decrease involuntary memories and that measures of involuntary and voluntary

36   memory were positively correlated. In Experiment 2, we focused specifically on

37   replicating the effect of the visuospatial intervention on involuntary memories of a

38   trauma film. We did not replicate this effect: results provided substantial Bayesian

39   evidence against an effect of the intervention. Overall, our findings provide evidence

40   against the DRA as a viable theoretical account of intrusive memories, at least in the

41   context of a laboratory analogue of trauma.

45   Public Significance Statement

46

47   This research shows that a widely discussed theory about posttraumatic stress

48   disorder, which provides an account as to why traumatic memories intrude into

49   everyday life, may not accurately explain those experiences, at least when tested

50   under controlled laboratory conditions with analogue trauma. The findings point to a

51   need to rethink how intrusive memories work and how trauma may impact our ability

52   to deliberately remember an experience. Moreover, they challenge previous evidence

53   that a simple visuospatial task can reduce immediate lab-based measures of intrusive

54   memories.

55

56   Keywords: intrusive memories, involuntary memory, trauma film paradigm,

57   posttraumatic stress disorder

59          The dual representation account (Brewin et al., 2010; henceforth, DRA) for

60   posttraumatic stress disorder proposes that during a traumatic event, intense

61   emotional arousal up-modulates activity in a network comprising the amygdala, insula

62   and early sensory cortical and subcortical areas, which strengthens the encoding of

63   affective and sensory elements of the event. This explains why following the traumatic

64   event, intrusive memories typically take the form of vivid mental images such as sights

65   and sounds. At the same time, emotional arousal downregulates processing in

66   hippocampal, parahippocampal and other structures in the medial temporal lobe which

67   implement associative binding of the different elements of the episode (Figure 1). This

68   results in ineffective integration of the affective and sensory elements with the

69   spatiotemporal contextual details of the event, resulting in a fragmented episodic

70   memory (Brewin et al., 2010). Together, these changes are thought to explain why

71   sensory cues and feelings that are similar to aspects of the traumatic event can trigger

72   unwanted involuntary memories of the event (henceforth: intrusive memories) even in

73   spatiotemporally-dissimilar contexts. The core assumption of the DRA is that there are

74   in effect two memory traces – sensory and contextualised representations – and that

75   “pathological” encoding of trauma strengthens the former but weakens the latter,

76   leading to dissociations between involuntary and voluntary memory.


77          The DRA was inspired by clinical findings that intrusive memories in

78   posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD, American Psychiatric Association 2013) are

79   often fragmented (Brewin et al., 1996; for an overview see McNally, 2022), although

80   the adequacy of this observation has been questioned (Rubin et al., 2008). One

81   approach to studying intrusive memories in the laboratory uses emotionally distressing

82   films as an experimental analogue of trauma exposure. In this “trauma film” paradigm,

83   participants encode film clips that depict scenes with traumatic content, such as “actual
                                                                                            5


84    or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence”, following from the DSM-5

85    definition of trauma (American Psychiatric Association, 2013), under conditions that

86    encourage personal involvement and immersion. Healthy participants typically report

87    that segments of the films come to mind involuntarily for several days after viewing

88    such films (e.g. Kobelt et al., 2024; Weidmann, Conradi, Gröger, Fehm & Fydrich,

89    2009; for review, see James et al., 2016). Same-day lab-based measures have also

90    been used rather than just diary methods in real life (James et al., 2015; Lau-Zhu et

91    al., 2019, 2021).


92           This evidence suggests that the trauma film paradigm can act as a laboratory

93    model of exposure to trauma and subsequent intrusive memories of the experimental

94    trauma, e.g. in PTSD (James et al., 2016). Brewin has recently emphasized that

95    despite the difference between real trauma and lab analogues of trauma, the trauma

96    film paradigm “has been very productive… in situations that demonstrate significant

97    effects among participants all exposed to such a film. For example, it has provided

98    evidence of a robust dissociation between the effects of a visuospatial intervention on

99    voluntary and involuntary memory of traumatic scenes” (Brewin & Field, 2024, p. 2).

100   This quote suggests that as long as the trauma film paradigm elicits intrusions in daily

101   life, it provides a good model of pathological encoding, even in healthy participants.

102   Indeed, it would be odd if there were two separate mechanisms in the brain that

103   generate intrusive memories.


104          Crucially, the frequency of intrusions reported in the trauma film paradigm has

105   been found to be unrelated to the accuracy of voluntary recall of the same material

106   (Holmes et al., 2004), capturing the aforementioned characteristic of PTSD, i.e., that

107   voluntary and involuntary recall can dissociate. Furthermore, certain visuospatial
                                                                                          6


108   interventions performed after film-viewing selectively decrease intrusive memory, but

109   do not appear to decrease voluntary memory for the same films (Asselbergs et al.,

110   2024; Deeprose et al., 2012; Holmes et al., 2009, 2010; James et al., 2015; Kessler

111   et al., 2020; Lau-Zhu et al., 2019, 2021). The DRA has been used to interpret the

112   results of the trauma film paradigm, especially findings of decreased involuntary

113   memory for emotionally negative, arousing content following a visuospatial

114   interference task (James et al., 2015; Lau-Zhu et al., 2019, 2021). The functional

115   dissociation between voluntary and involuntary memory, which we refer to as the

116   ‘selective interference effect’, provides particularly strong support for the DRA. For

117   example, James et al. (2015) state that their "procedure modified intrusion frequency

118   while leaving recognition memory intact, which indicates that trauma-film memory had

119   not been erased but ceased intruding involuntarily. Although consistent with clinical

120   models (Brewin, 2014), the dissociation between intrusions and recognition is contrary

121   to traditional memory models (e.g., Tulving, 2002)—a paradox requiring future

122   research."

141         By positing two separate traces following pathological encoding of trauma, the

142   core assumption of the DRA contrasts with a core mainstream assumption in memory

143   research, which considers that all manifestations of episodic memory – both voluntary
                                                                                          8


144   and involuntary – originate from a single hippocampus index that binds together all of

145   its elements, including its spatiotemporal context and the multimodal sensory

146   representations (Frankland & Bontempi, 2005). We refer to this view as the ‘unitary’

147   account of memory. Specifically, the assumption of two separate traces in the DRA

148   contrasts with unitary conceptualisations of PTSD that consider intrusive memory and

149   voluntary memories to be based on the same memory trace (Berntsen & Rubin, 2008;

150   Cohen & Kahana, 2022; Rubin et al., 2008). For example, a recent formal model of

151   emotional memory, which was developed to explain recall of neutral words in

152   laboratory experiments, has been applied to emotional memory and to intrusive

153   memory in mental ill-health (Cohen & Kahana, 2022; Talmi et al., 2019). The model

154   simulates each item or experience as a one-hot vector, the neural machinery that

155   implements memory in the brain as a two-layer neural network and encoding and

156   retrieval processes as operations carried out within this network. The emotional

157   Context Maintenance and Retrieval (eCMR) and CMR3 models simulate emotional

158   experiences through the value of the elements in the vectors that represent the

159   experience. These models are supported by their ability to predict patterns of results

160   in the human experimental and clinical psychology literature. Crucially for our

161   purposes, CMR3 explicitly simulates the retrieval of experiences in the same way

162   regardless of whether retrieval was intentional or involuntary.


163          Evidence that supports the unitary account of emotional memory includes the

164   robust finding that voluntary memory of emotionally-arousing stimuli is enhanced when

165   tested with methods that are known to tap on to hippocampal memory traces, such as

166   free recall (Talmi, Luk, et al., 2007; Talmi, Schimmack, et al., 2007), cued recall

167   (Dolcos et al., 2004) and recollection (Mihaylova et al., 2019; Rimmele et al., 2011,

168   2012; Yonelinas & Ritchey, 2015). The effects of emotion on voluntary memory are
                                                                                              9


169   attributed to emotional intensity rather than valence or the type of the emotion (Mather

170   et al., 2015). Additionally, in two studies of paired-associate cued recall, activation in

171   the hippocampus predicted recall of both negative and neutral stimuli, i.e., was not

172   modulated by the valence of the material (Bisby et al., 2016; Madan et al., 2017). The

173   findings that hippocampal-dependent memory for laboratory stimuli that are negative

174   and arousing is enhanced, rather than being impoverished, contradict the predictions

175   derived from the extension of DRA to milder emotional stimuli, and by so doing,

176   support the unitary account.


177          It is possible that the relatively mild manipulation of emotion in the

178   aforementioned studies was insufficient to weaken associative binding. However,

179   these studies fall within the scope of the extended explanatory scope of the DRA, as

180   it is currently used in emotional memory research. For example, in their joint review,

181   Bisby, Burgess and Brewin (2020, p. 270) show “how negative events can disrupt

182   these processes in healthy individuals and have argued that these mechanisms are

183   impaired in PTSD, resulting in impaired binding and holistic retrieval in this disorder.

184   Studies investigating such basic mechanisms provide a valuable addition to clinical

185   studies of trauma narratives and will… be crucial in providing further insight into

186   memory disruptions in PTSD”. That said, to test predictions derived from the DRA, it

187   may be necessary to employ stimuli that evoke stronger emotions, such as those used

188   in the trauma film paradigm.


189          Experiment 1 was conducted to test three key predictions derived from the DRA

190   using the trauma film paradigm. This paradigm was employed in a between-

191   participants design, with the factors of intentionality of retrieval (voluntary vs.

192   involuntary memory), emotion (traumatic vs. neutral film content) and intervention
                                                                                              10


193   (visuospatial interference vs. auditory control task). Prior to either intervention, we

194   presented participants with reminder cues of the film, such that the reminder occurred

195   in both intervention conditions. In the visuospatial interference intervention, the

196   reminder was followed by the computer game of Tetris, which participants play under

197   instructions to utilise mental rotation. This type of procedure has been shown to reduce

198   intrusive memories in a number of previous studies (Asselbergs et al., 2024; Badawi

199   et al., 2020; Deeprose et al., 2012; Holmes et al., 2009, 2010; James et al., 2015;

200   Kessler et al., 2020; Lau-Zhu et al., 2019, 2021). However, it is important to note that

201   other studies with possible variations on the intervention procedure have not observed

202   a reduction in intrusions (Asselbergs et al., 2018; Brennen et al., 2021; Broughill et al.,

203   2025; Brühl et al., 2019; Matura et al., 2025), while others show mixed effects, such

204   as a reduction in shorter term but not in longer term intrusive memories (Wessel et al.,

205   2025). A recent meta-analysis of laboratory studies shows that, on balance, this type

206   of procedure decreased the frequency of intrusions (Varma et al., 2024).


207          Our main measure of voluntary and involuntary memory was the number of

208   memory reports in a Vigilance Retrieval Task (VRT) designed to closely replicate the

209   task employed by Lau-Zhu et al. (2019, 2021) on the same day as film viewing. After

210   watching a film, participants in the VRT perform a primary digit-monitoring go-no go

211   task, which demands most of their attention. Occasionally, blurred frames from the

212   preceding target film and from an unseen foil film are presented behind the digits. This

213   is supposed to mimic memory intrusions in everyday life, when one’s attention is

214   directed elsewhere, but a cue resembling a prior experience triggers the unintentional

215   retrieval of a memory for that experience. The participants’ secondary task is to press

216   a key when such a memory comes to mind, at which point the primary task stops and

217   they are asked to describe that memory. The main outcome variables are 1) the total
                                                                                             11


218   number of button presses (intrusions) and, by analysing the memory descriptions, 2)

219   the number of unique film details reported, excluding repetitions of recurring details.

220   As is typical with voluntary recall of films descriptions were coded according to the

221   Autobiographical Interview (Levine et al., 2002). This is the first time such scoring is

222   applied to analyse involuntary memory data after a trauma film. As is typical for trauma

223   film studies, participants also completed diaries over the subsequent few days,

224   recording intrusions from the films that occurred during their everyday lives, as an

225   adjunctive measure of involuntary memory, albeit briefer to reduce burden, since it

226   was not our main focus. The diary data were used as a manipulation check and

227   supplementary measure for comparison with previous studies.

242   Hypothesis A: Visuospatial interference decreases involuntary memory for

243   traumatic film content more than voluntary memory

244          This hypothesis is based on a core claim of the DRA: “The extent of voluntary

245   recall and the number of involuntary images of the same traumatic materials appear

246   to be functionally independent” (Brewin, 2014, p. 85). In this section, we query which

247   dependent variable operationalises this claim most appropriately. The literature on

248   involuntary autobiographical memories distinguishes between the number of unique

249   memories that come to mind and the frequency with which they do so (Berntsen &

250   Rubin, 2008). Similarly, in the trauma film paradigm, one can measure the number of

251   unique intrusive memories (e.g., from different scenes in the film), or their total

252   number, including those with repeated content. For example, if a participant has three

253   intrusions in total, two that are the same and relate to event A and another that relates

254   to event B, they will be scored as having three intrusive memories (henceforth: DV1)

255   but only two unique ones (henceforth: DV2). Typically, only a small proportion of total

256   intrusions reflect unique memories. This is because only a few ‘hotspots’ in the films

257   are perceived as the “worst moments” for any one individual, but memories of the

258   same hotspots may come to mind many times. The measure of total intrusions has

259   greater clinical significance as an analogue of the extent of intrusive memory causing

260   disruption in PTSD and therefore often forms the key dependent variable in the trauma

261   film paradigm. This is why, in most previous studies that used diaries to record

262   intrusions, participants were instructed to record every intrusion in their week-long

263   diary, even if from the same memory. By contrast, the typical measure in voluntary

264   memory tests is a count of unique studied items that participants can recall or

265   recognise among foils. This raises the possibility that the differential effect of the
                                                                                              14


266   intervention could be due to using different measures in the involuntary and voluntary

267   conditions.

268          To appreciate the significance of this observation, consider that the voluntary

269   memory measures in most previous studies of the trauma film paradigm were cued

270   recall or recognition tests, where it is impossible for participants to repeatedly retrieve

271   the same stimuli. Repetitions are, in principle, possible in free recall tests of memory,

272   but are often not reported. In practice, free recall tests encourage “retrieval

273   monitoring”, defined as the search and decision processes operating during

274   recollection attempts (Koriat & Goldsmith, 1996). Consequently, participants do not

275   typically repeat what they have already said (Zaromb et al., 2006).

276          In addition to distinguishing two types of memory performance (DV1 and DV2),

277   we changed three aspects of the previous free recall protocol of Lau-Zhu and

278   colleagues (2019) to better match the voluntary and involuntary measures. First, both

279   voluntary and involuntary memory measures were collected under the matched

280   conditions of the VRT. While previous studies tested involuntary memory using free

281   report (of intrusions), most of these studies tested voluntary memory using recognition

282   (e.g. Holmes et al., 2009), a measure that does not necessarily entail hippocampal-

283   dependent associative retrieval (e.g., Yonelinas et al., 2001). In the only study to test

284   both voluntary and involuntary memory using free report, voluntary memory was tested

285   under full attention conditions, but involuntary memory was tested under the dual-task

286   conditions of the VRT (Lau-Zhu et al., 2019). Second, our instructions encouraged

287   participants not to censor repetitions in either involuntary or voluntary conditions. This

288   was helped by the fact that memories were reported by typing into a text box, which

289   disappeared when they had finished, so participants could not refer back to what they

290   already retrieved (cf. previous studies using written recall on paper). Third, we
                                                                                              15


291   manipulated retrieval intentionality between-participants. The two studies that reported

292   the key dissociation between voluntary and involuntary memory using the VRT (Lau-

293   Zhu et al., 2019, 2021) used a within-participant design, where the involuntary test

294   was given first, followed by the voluntary test. This not only results in unequal retention

295   intervals, but also output interference, where the reports from the first test interfere

296   with reports from the second test. A within-participant design may also encourage

297   adoption of various strategies in the second test, such as explicit attempts to recall

298   items reported in the first test or to suppress such items.

299          To summarise, we extended the previous studies cited above that reported a

300   dissociation between the effect of visuospatial intervention on voluntary and

301   involuntary memory by making important methodological improvements to a

302   laboratory measure of intrusions following the trauma film paradigm. Using the VRT,

303   we scored both the total number and number of unique memories, equated the

304   memory cues and level of attention, matched the retrieval instructions, and eliminated

305   test order effects. The functional dissociation reported in previous studies could have

306   arisen from one or more of these methodological differences between voluntary and

307   involuntary conditions. We aimed to test a key prediction derived from the DRA, that

308   a visuospatial intervention decreases involuntary but not voluntary memory on the

309   VRT. While replicating the previous functional dissociation would bolster support for

310   the DRA, a lack of such an interaction would favour unitary accounts of voluntary and

311   involuntary memory.

313   Hypothesis B: Visuospatial Interference decreases involuntary memory more in

314   the traumatic than the neutral content condition
                                                                                              16


315          In his seminal 2014 paper, Brewin attributes the functional dissociation between

316   the effect of a visuospatial intervention (vs. the control intervention) on involuntary vs.

317   voluntary memory for traumatic experiences to dysfunctional encoding during trauma

318   that is unlikely during neutral experiences. This claim is central to the present paper,

319   so it is worthwhile to quote in full: “Engaging in visuospatial tasks at or shortly after

320   trauma encoding appears to block the development of involuntary images, whereas

321   engaging in a verbal task that specifically involves counting backward leads to more

322   involuntary images; the extent of voluntary recall and the number of involuntary images

323   of the same traumatic materials appear to be functionally independent… These data

324   are very largely based on viewing of trauma films, and the effects may not be observed

325   with other stimuli… stimuli that have no specific personal meaning may make it unlikely

326   that dissociation, a typical response to traumatic situations, will occur” (Brewin, 2014,

327   p. 85). This statement clearly implies that predictions derived from the DRA entail that

328   we should only observe a significant interaction between intentionality and the

329   intervention in the trauma condition, but not in the neutral condition.

330          To quote again from Brewin, “visuospatial tasks are particularly likely to

331   interfere with perceptual, image-based representations” (Brewin, 2014, p. 80). The

332   DRA considers involuntary memories of trauma, either in PTSD or in the trauma film

333   paradigm, to be image-based. Supporting the possibility that they are particularly

334   image-based, one previous laboratory study has compared the phenomenology of

335   involuntary memories for trauma and neutral content, and found that involuntary

336   memory of the trauma content was more image-based than verbal-based (Hagenaars

337   et al., 2010).

338          Involuntary memories also follow non-trauma experiences and are common in

339   our daily lives (Berntsen, 2021). Outside the lab, negative involuntary memories are
                                                                                           17


340   prevalent in many mental health conditions, where they are often associated with a

341   range of stressful events (e.g. a moment of being deeply shamed by peers at school

342   associated with depression), not just DSM-5 trauma, and positive involuntary

343   memories have been reported in, for example, hypomania and food cravings (Brewin

344   et al., 2010; Holmes & Mathews, 2010; Malik et al., 2014). In the lab, involuntary

345   memories have also been documented following positive and depressive film material

346   (Clark et al., 2013; Davies et al., 2012; Lang et al., 2009). Involuntary memories for

347   non-trauma content can also be image-based. This possibility is supported by findings

348   that involuntary memories were rated as clearer and more vivid than voluntary

349   memories, and that some of them were rated as more relevant to the task situation in

350   which they came to mind (Barzykowski & Staugaard, 2016). To the extent that

351   involuntary memories in our neutral condition are image-based, then based on the

352   DRA, the visuospatial intervention could decrease them.

353          Taken together, these two quotes lead to apparently contradictory inferences

354   about what the DRA would predict for our neutral condition. The first (trauma-based)

355   suggests that a visuospatial intervention would decrease involuntary memory only in

356   the trauma condition, while the second (image-based) suggests that it would decrease

357   neutral involuntary memory as well, consistent with the findings of Davies et al (2012)

358   for involuntary memory after a positive film.

359          The DRA has not considered neutral involuntary memories much, and this may

360   be the reason for the apparent contradiction. This is because according to the DRA,

361   there would be more involuntary memory following a traumatic than a neutral

362   experience. A core assumption of the DRA is that pathological encoding of a trauma

363   weakens    hippocampal-dependent       contextual   representations,   so   that   these

364   representations do not inhibit the automatic, unwanted retrieval of sensory
                                                                                            18


365   representations that are routinely cued by the environment. By contrast, memories of

366   less emotional experiences are less likely to be encoded in a pathological way and

367   are, hence, integrated with their spatiotemporal context and less likely to come to

368   mind. If the number of involuntary memories in the trauma condition is very different

369   than in the neutral condition in our control condition, and visuospatial intervention has

370   the same proportional impact in both conditions, it would decrease the number of

371   voluntary memories more in the trauma condition. Taken together, we argue that

372   based on the DRA, an interaction would be expected between the factors of

373   intervention and emotion, owing to proportional effects of the intervention applied to

374   different baselines.

375          To test this prediction and illuminate the debate, we adapted the trauma film

376   paradigm for viewing neutral films without traumatic content. We opted to use films

377   that most participants experience as neutral because in most past studies, the non-

378   trauma films were still emotionally evocative, and therefore, it is possible that strong

379   arousal up-modulated their sensory representations (Mather et al., 2015); even if this

380   effect has been particularly apparent for negative content (Bowen et al., 2018). We

381   administered the neutral and the traumatic films to separate groups of participants,

382   half of whom received the visuospatial intervention and half the control intervention.

383   Our predictions derived from the DRA are that there will be more involuntary memories

384   following the trauma condition compared to the neutral condition, and that emotion

385   and intervention would interact, such that the visuospatial intervention will decrease

386   the number of involuntary memories more in the trauma condition.

387          Finding that a visuospatial intervention decreases involuntary memories to the

388   same extent regardless of their emotionality would challenge the DRA by decreasing

389   the case for evoking separate representations for trauma situations specifically, given
                                                                                          19


390   the DRA was at heart derived to account for the pathological encoding of trauma. Such

391   a finding would instead be consistent with a unitary account of memory. It would

392   suggest that the functional dissociation between the effect of the visuospatial

393   intervention on voluntary and involuntary memory reported in previous research is not

394   due to quasi-pathological encoding of trauma but applies just as well to healthy

395   encoding of neutral content. Furthermore, it would suggest that there may not be

396   mechanistic differences between involuntary memory in everyday life and intrusive

397   memory in PTSD, as assumed by some researchers. As such, it would help evolve

398   the unitary account of memory by encouraging research on how a single trace can

399   support both voluntary and involuntary memories, both neutral and traumatic.

400   Likewise, it might also help evolve future versions of DRA, which has not yet been

401   extended to account for neutral memory.

403   Hypothesis C: Trauma decreases voluntary memory

404         Because intact hippocampal function is essential for the ability to freely recall

405   previously-studied verbal and visual stimuli (Isaac & Mayes, 1999a, 1999b), and a

406   core assumption of the DRA is that pathological encoding of trauma weakens

407   hippocampal-dependent contextual representations, it follows that based on the DRA,

408   pathological encoding of trauma would impair voluntary free recall, while healthy

409   encoding of trauma or of non-trauma content would enhance voluntary recall. This is

410   clear, for example, in this quote: “According to dual representation theory, perception

411   of a moderately stressful or emotionally salient event results in the creation of more

412   enduring C-reps and S-reps.” (Brewin et al., 2010, p.221). The balance between

413   healthy and quasi-pathological encoding in the sample allocated to the voluntary
                                                                                            20


414   trauma condition will determine whether average recall performance in that condition

415   is lower or higher than performance in the voluntary neutral condition.

416          In their 2020 review, Bisby, Brewin and Burgess discussed experimental

417   paradigms that contrast voluntary memory for negative and neutral content and argued

418   that lower associative memory performance in the negative condition reflects

419   mechanisms that are impaired in PTSD. This suggests that healthy samples tend to

420   encode even milder emotional situations in a quasi-pathological way, measurable in

421   indices of associative memory. Based on this extension of the DRA to milder emotional

422   situations we can derive a more clear-cut prediction: that recall of trauma films will be

423   decreased, compared to films with neutral content. This interpretative framework is

424   gaining dominance in emotional memory research (e.g., Clewett & McClay, 2024).

425          The robust finding of enhanced free recall of emotional material appears to

426   challenge this extension of the DRA. It has been robustly demonstrated that when

427   people view emotional and neutral stimuli, such as words and images, they typically

428   recall more of the emotional than neutral stimuli (Ack-Baraly et al., 2016; Talmi, 2013).

429   However, as mentioned before, any effects in these studies may be due to the

430   relatively mild emotional arousal they induced in participants. The trauma film

431   paradigm employs specific instructions to increase engagement with the film content

432   to enhance it utility as an analogue of exposure to trauma. Brewin and Field (2024)

433   suggest that the presence of intrusive memories in setups that resemble the current

434   involuntary trauma condition is indicative that some pathological encoding has

435   occurred.

436          The prediction derived from the extension of the DRA therefore suggests that

437   voluntary recall of trauma content will be decreased, compared to recall of neutral

438   content, because free recall relies on associative processes, and these should be
                                                                                           21


439   weaker when content is emotionally arousing and negative. Failure to observe this

440   pattern will help develop the DRA by clarifying how it applies to milder emotional

441   manipulations.

442          All our conclusions will be inevitably tempered by the degree to which our

443   outcome variables reflect each type of memory representation accurately and reliably,

444   but this limitation is particularly acute for this latter prediction where the degree of

445   pathological encoding may depend on stimulus and individual differences. Given the

446   nuance involved in developing Hypothesis C, we treat it as having secondary

447   importance compared to Hypotheses A and B. We therefore planned to only terminate

448   our sequential design based on our primary Hypotheses A and B (see Methods).

449

450   Hypothesis D: Involuntary memory will not predict subsequent voluntary

451   memory of the trauma film

452          Examining both voluntary and involuntary within the same participants allows

453   us to test another prediction of the DRA. The DRA contends that there is a “functional

454   independence between perceptual and episodic memory systems” (Brewin, 2014,

455   p.80). This contention is supported by evidence that voluntary and involuntary memory

456   are not correlated across participants (e.g., Tables 3 and 4 in Brewin, 2014). Our study

457   provided an opportunity to examine this question afresh, with the benefit that the

458   voluntary measures of memory is more closely matched to the involuntary measure

459   than in previous studies, and that we extract two dependent measures, typical to each

460   research literature. Thus, our fourth hypothesis (Hypothesis D, Table 1) was that

461   voluntary and involuntary memory would not be correlated across participants. Since

462   Brewin criticised studies that employed a voluntary test prior to the involuntary test,

463   and that administering a voluntary memory test for a trauma film has been shown to
                                                                                            22


464   decrease subsequent involuntary memory reports (Krans et al., 2009), we only

465   examined this correlation on the half of the sample who undertook the involuntary test

466   first.

468                                          Methods
469            This in-person laboratory study used a 2x2x2 between-participants factorial

470   design, with factors of intentionality (voluntary vs. involuntary memory), emotion (films

471   with trauma vs. neutral content) and intervention (visuospatial interference vs. control

472   task). Participants were randomly allocated to one of the eight conditions.

473            To compare involuntary (intrusion) and voluntary memories of film content most

474   effectively, the task used should be tightly matched between both conditions. A tight

475   match ensures that the previously reported dissociation between intrusions and

476   voluntary memories cannot be traced back to differences in task parameters. Previous

477   dissociations between intrusive and voluntary memory have been based on very

478   distinct tests, such as a diary (intrusion; mostly taken over 7 days in the participants’

479   daily life) versus lab-based tests for voluntary memory (recognition memory, which

480   involves a lower element of search; or free recall). The Vigilance Retrieval Task (VRT),

481   developed by Lau-Zhu and colleagues to measure intrusive memory (Lau-Zhu et al.,

482   2019, 2021) and here extended to also measure voluntary memory, permits closer

483   matching of these parameters. This is because the VRT can be administered in the

484   same environment (lab), with the same number and timings of memory cues (blurred

485   images from the films distributed throughout the task), involves the same primary task

486   (number counting) and output format of memories (typed). We thus used the VRT as

487   a primary outcome measure for both involuntary and voluntary memories in a
                                                                                           23


488   between-subject design (to keep the retention interval the same). Following this part

489   of the experiment, participants undertook a second VRT, as described below.

490          We also included a diary measure, for the purpose of allowing a comparison to

491   previous studies of intrusive memories (since diary is the usual primary outcome in

492   such studies). The intrusive memory diary was conducted online rather than on paper,

493   as is increasingly common in research in this area (Espinosa et al., 2023; Singh et al.,

494   2023). Given our focus on the VRT, the total duration of the lab-based session, and

495   the high recruitment target, we opted for a briefer version of the diary with minimal

496   demands on participants (on the day of the experimental session and the following 3

497   instead of 7 days), and single rather than multiple prompts per day (1 time slot per day

498   rather than the usual 3). We chose this time-period because intrusions tend to be

499   highest in the first three days following the intervention (James et al., 2015) and to

500   only have one time slot to reduce participant burden.

501          It is important to manipulate intentionality of retrieval between-participants,

502   because otherwise, and as discussed above, test order effects, such as those due to

503   output interference, may confound the results. However, it is also advantageous to

504   measure voluntary and involuntary memory within the same participants, because the

505   specific hotspots that an individual experiences as “worst moments”, and therefore

506   later become intrusive memories, vary across individuals (Holmes et al., 2005; Grey

507   & Holmes, 2008). Thus, in an experimental context one way to tell whether a particular

508   event was a hotspot for someone is to check whether it comes to mind in an involuntary

509   memory test. It follows that we can only examine voluntary memory for hotspots if we

510   collect both voluntary and involuntary measures in the same participant. Although

511   previous comparisons between voluntary and involuntary memory measured each

512   type of memory separately, rather than conditionalize one measure on the other, it is
                                                                                            24


513   theoretically important to do so because the voluntary/involuntary dissociation

514   predicted by DRA specifically concerns memory for the hotspots themselves. In

515   particular, the DRA prediction that a visuospatial intervention affects involuntary but

516   not voluntary memories (Hypothesis B) is arguably restricted to hotspots within the film

517   (since non-traumatic, non-hotspot moments should not intrude). It is possible that

518   previous evidence of this dissociation may be due to voluntary recall measures being

519   dominated by details from non-hotspots, which were not affected by the intervention.

520   It is therefore possible that if we restrict the memory measure to hotspots, the

521   intervention may affect involuntary and voluntary recall equivalently. Such a finding

522   will challenge an important support for the DRA. These considerations led us to ask

523   all participants to undertake two tests of memory in the VRT, a voluntary and an

524   involuntary test, one after the other (their order counter-balanced across participants).

525   The key Hypotheses A-C (Table 1) were tested only with data from the first VRT, so

526   are immune to the effects of test order, but we also analysed the second test for

527   Hypothesis D.

528          The trauma film paradigm has been argued to be a “productive” laboratory

529   analogue of trauma (Brewin & Field, 2024), but changing the protocol could weaken

530   this support. For example, Brewin and Field (p. 2) argue that “When findings are null...

531   the paradigm is vulnerable to the criticism that this may be because it does not

532   adequately model exposure to a traumatic event.” For this reason, we kept three

533   features of the typical protocol. First, we manipulated the intervention task between-

534   participants to avoid potential order effects in terms of the impact of the trauma or

535   demand characteristics (Kehyayan et al., 2024). Second, we manipulated film content

536   between-participants to keep the nature of the experience as similar as possible to the

537   original (and use the same trauma films as those used in Lau-Zhu et al., 2019, 2021).
                                                                                         25


538   Finally, we collected diary measures of involuntary memory to strengthen evidence

539   that we have adequately modelled exposure to a traumatic event.

565            To determine the sample size in Experiment 1 we used a sequential Bayesian

566   design (Schönbrodt & Wagenmakers, 2018), planning to stop when the Bayes Factor

567   (BF) was greater than 6 for either the alternative (BF10) or null (BF01) model for each

568   of our two main hypotheses (A and B), using DV2. To minimise “false positives”, we

569   planned to collect initial batches of n=30 in each of the 8 groups, followed by batches

570   of n=10 per group if our BF criteria were not met, up to a maximum of n=60 per group

571   (i.e., N=480 participants in total) for practical (resource) reasons. Previous trauma film

572   paradigm studies have estimated a main effect size of the intervention (compared to

573   control) on involuntary memory with Cohen’s d=.91 (Lau-Zhu et al., 2019; based on

574   diary data from Holmes et al., 2009). The effect of the intervention on the VRT in Lau-

575   Zhu et al. (2019) was 0.96 and 1.20 in Experiments 2 and 3, respectively. Given that

576   neither interactions of the intervention with emotionality of the film nor intentionality in

577   the VRT have been tested thus far, we simulated interaction effects of approximately

578   half this size, i.e. Cohen’s d of 0.5 (f2=0.06). If both hypotheses A and B have this

579   effect size, simulations with the anovaBF function showed 78% probability of finding

580   BF10>6 for both (with median of 50 per group after stopping), or >99.9% probability of

581   finding such evidence for at least one of these hypotheses. If both of the two null

582   hypotheses were true, then at the maximum N, the “false positive” rate was importantly

583   low at 1.3%. However, in a deviation from our pre-registration, a coding error meant

584   that the script instructed us to stop when BF01>3 rather than BF01>6. With this more

585   liberal threshold, we had a 55% chance of supporting both nulls, or 89% chance of

586   supporting at least one null.
                                                                                            27


587         To minimise any potential bias during data analysis, at each stopping point, an

588   automated analysis script was used, which provided only either a ‘stop’ or ‘go’ answer

589   but not the BFs associated with each hypothesis (Sadibolova & Terhune, 2025).

604   Materials
605         Each film consisted of 11 audiovisual clips sourced from the public domain

606   and had a total duration of 12 minutes. The trauma film was the same as in our

607   previous studies (Lau-Zhu et al., 2019, 2021) and featured content showing types of

608   events listed as traumatic events in the DSM-5, including road safety films featuring

609   car accidents, scenes of war, and surgical close ups. The films selected for the

610   neutral condition included non-threatening content, such as cooking, driving and
                                                                                              28


611   using an exercise machine. The films were presented on a wall projector in a

612   darkened room, with sound delivered via headphones.

613          In previous studies employing neutral and negative film paradigms, some

614   authors selected films that differed primarily in emotional tone without systematically

615   matching other stimulus characteristics (e.g., Chouinard-Gaouette & Blanchette,

616   2025; Hørlyck et al., 2019), while others additionally controlled thematic content

617   (e.g., traffic vs. food scenes) and film length (Arnaudova & Hagenaars, 2017; Ferree

618   & Cahill, 2009; Hagenaars et al., 2010; Kobelt et al., 2024). More superficial visual

619   attributes are more rarely controlled (Arnaudova & Hagenaars, 2017; Kobelt et al.,

620   2024), as are narrative qualities (Ferree & Cahill, 2009, Taylor et al., 2021; 2025).

621   Here, we matched the number and duration of clips in the negative and neutral films

622   and included people in every clip. Instead of content, we matched the semantic

623   relatedness of the clips in the trauma and neutral condition, a variable with known

624   influence on memory recall (Talmi & Moscovitch, 2004).

625          The emotional and neutral films were matched based on ratings solicited from

626   online pilot participants (n = 19; 10 females, 9 males; age M = 33.25, SD = 11.97).

627   As expected, emotional clips were rated as more negative than neutral clips (M =

628   6.99, SD = 0.68 vs. M = 4.23, SD = 0.64), with a Bayesian t-test providing extreme

629   evidence for this difference, BF10 = 6.19 x 106. Emotional clips were also significantly

630   more arousing (M = 5.66, SD = 1.45) than neutral clips (M = 2.56, SD = 1.10), with a

631   Bayesian t-test providing extreme evidence for this difference, BF10 = 4.40 x 104.

632   Emotional clips were rated as having a significantly higher subjective impact (M =

633   5.68, SD = 1.59) than neutral clips (M = 2.40, SD = 1.05), with a Bayesian t-test

634   providing extreme evidence for this difference, BF10 = 2.20 x 106. These results

635   supported the differentiation of the two stimulus sets. Finally, pairs of film clips were
                                                                                                 29


636   rated for similarity on a 1-7 scale. Inter-rater reliability of similarity judgments was

637   assessed using an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Reliability was good for

638   neutral clip pairs (ICC = 0.80) and excellent for emotional clip pairs (ICC = 0.91),

639   indicating strong agreement across participants. The final selected 11 clips from

640   each Emotion condition had average similarity scores of 3.97 in the Negative and

641   3.94 in the Neutral condition, with a paired Bayesian t-test providing moderate

642   evidence against a difference in similarity (BF01 = 4.17).

643          The reminder cues consisted of 11 film stills. One still was taken from each of

644   the discrete clips in the film and presented in the same fixed order as the scenes

645   within the film. The stills for the traumatic content condition are equivalent to Lau-Zhu

646   et al. (2019), and correspond to frames just before the worst moments, which have

647   been clinically associated with intrusive memories (Ehlers, Hackmann, & Michael,

648   2004). Examples are: a picture of a circus (prior to the elephant escaping and going

649   on a rampage), or a smiling teenager (just before getting hit by a van while being

650   distracted by texting). For the neutral content condition, stills were taken from the

651   beginning of the film. Examples are: a picture of two women standing in a gym

652   (before starting a tutorial about a leg-press machine), or a teacher in front of a

653   classroom (where she goes on to teach her students about coordinate systems).

654          The images used in the VRT included blurred versions of 11 film stills (1 per

655   movie, featuring similar content to the reminder cues; each displayed twice) and 68

656   foil stills (novel images featuring a variety of coloured indoor/outdoor scenes; each

657   displayed once), shown in a fixed randomized order. The images were passed

658   through a Gaussian filter using the MATLAB function imgaussfilt (sigma = 38), to

659   approximate cues glimpsed in everyday life when outside one’s focus of attention

660   (Berntsen, 2009). The reminder cues and VRT were implemented using MATLAB
                                                                                            30


661   R2023b (Mathworks Inc., Sherbom, MA) running Psychtoolbox version 3 (Brainard,

662   1997; Pelli, 1997; Kleiner et al., 2007). The aspect of the VRT “please describe your

663   memory here” (Figure 2) involved participants typing a description of their memory

664   into an unconstrained text entry field as soon as they made each response (see

665   Appendix for instructions both voluntary and involuntary VRT).

666          The visuospatial intervention condition involved playing the game Tetris (The

667   Tetris Company, 2007). The game’s objective is to arrange different geometric objects

668   into complete horizontal lines. The objects are of different shape and colour, can be

669   rotated using the arrow key, and fall from the top of the screen. Whenever a full

670   horizontal line is formed, it disappears, and the participant is awarded points. The

671   game adapts to participants’ performance and is over when the screen has vertically

672   filled up with the blocks.

673          For the control condition, we selected a podcast listening task, where after the

674   reminder cue participants listened to a podcast (the start of an episode of the BBC The

675   Public Philosopher, https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b043wx2s) about voting in

676   elections and answered simple content questions. Critically, listening to a podcast

677   uses a different sensory modality than the largely visuospatial nature of Tetris. This is

678   important since the DRA emphasizes the visuospatial aspect (Brewin, 2014), which

679   means that any visual control task could be problematic. Additional advantages of the

680   podcast task were that, like the visuospatial intervention, it also employed a digital

681   activity from the public domain, and controlled for the time spent, nature of contact

682   with researcher, instructions for active engagement; therefore providing more

683   experimental control than passive rest. A translational advantage is that the podcast

684   task has real-world clinical relevance, since it was used (with different podcasts) as a

685   control when an imagery-based experimental intervention was delivered soon after
                                                                                              31


686   trauma in a hospital emergency department (Kanstrup et al., 2021) and for established

687   intrusive memories (Kanstrup et al., 2024). The limitation of the podcast task is that

688   the podcast and the visuospatial interventions have not been formally matched for

689   cognitive load. Therefore, a limitation of this experiment is that the control intervention

690   controls for some, but not all, aspects of the visuospatial intervention. Additionally, the

691   effects of verbal interventions are mixed in the literature; a verbal pub quiz task

692   (Holmes et al., 2010) increased intrusions, but a word game task (Hagenaars et al.,

693   2017) decreased intrusions, relative to rest.

761   Intervention

762          Most previous studies of the trauma film paradigm have not blinded the

763   experimenter to any of the manipulations. Here the experimenter was blinded to the

764   allocation of the participant to the Emotion condition, which could otherwise have led

765   to different demeanour. When we designed this experiment we believed that given

766   that the experimenter needed to be in the room and give instructions to ensure and

767   check participant comprehension and adherence to the protocol associated with the

768   intervention, they cannot be blinded to the actual intervention. To mitigate demand

769   characteristics, the experimenter only learned the intervention condition after the post-

770   film structured break, before the reminder cues were shown.

771          Participants in the visuospatial intervention first received instructions on how to

772   play the game, with the emphasis that they should mentally rotate the pieces to foresee

773   where they will fall, and to pay attention to the three upcoming objects displayed at the

774   right side of the screen. Both switching off the ghost piece and the mental rotation

775   instructions were used to encourage participants’ active use of mental rotation in the

776   game. Instructions were followed by 1 minute of practice, after which Tetris was played

777   in marathon mode (with 15 levels) for 10mins, with the sound off.
                                                                                             35


778          Participants in the control condition received instructions on how to listen to the

779   podcast, with the emphasis to focus on the ideas and the meaning. They then received

780   the question sheet and went through it with the experimenter. Participants listened to

781   1min of the podcast for practice, and then continued listening to the episode for a

782   further 10 min.

784   Vigilance Retrieval Task (VRT)

785          We used the VRT to assess voluntary and involuntary memory in participants,

786   with the instructions presented in Appendix. In previous studies, we have validated the

787   VRT by showing that the number of intrusive memories in the task was associated

788   with the number of intrusions in a 7-day diary and verified that brief reports provided

789   during the VRT described actual film content (Lau-Zhu et al., 2019, 2021).

790          Participants completed a sustained attention Go/NoGo task, in which a stream

791   of digits (1-9) was presented at the centre of the screen (250ms; 270 total trials).

792   Participants were asked to respond with a button press to all digits, except for when a

793   ‘3’ was shown (11% of trials). The digits were either shown on a black background

794   (67% of trials) or a blurred image (33% of trials). While participants were instructed to

795   focus on the Go/NoGo aspect of the task, they were also asked to react with a separate

796   button press whenever they remembered any of the clips from the previous film. In the

797   involuntary condition, participants were asked to pause the task (by pressing a key)

798   whenever a “mental image [of the film] pops into your mind spontaneously”. The

799   voluntary condition was matched to the involuntary condition, except participants were

800   asked to deliberately recall film content and pause the task whenever they succeeded

801   (meeting the “retrieval intentionality criterion”; Schacter et al., 1989). Importantly,

802   between the two versions of the task, only the instructions differed (see Appendix).
                                                                                           36


803           Once participants had pressed the button to report, they were then asked to

804   provide further details of their memory (“Describe what you see or hear”). To make

805   their entry they typed into an unconstrained text entry field. For definitions of

806   involuntary and voluntary memory and instructions for giving the written descriptions

807   see Appendix. Without any memory reports, the task had a duration of 9 minutes. With

808   the memory reports the task duration increased to approximately 20 minutes. Each

809   participant completed both voluntary and involuntary versions of the VRT, but testing

810   order varied between participants.

812   Film ratings

813           Participants were presented with stills from each of the clips in the film and

814   asked to “judge whether you feel the content of the video created an instant sense of

815   impact on you personally” on a 0-9 sliding scale ranging from ‘no impact’ to ‘strong

816   impact’ (Croucher et al., 2011), followed by rating the clips on valence and arousal,

817   using the Self-Assessment Manikin scale (Bradley & Lang, 1994). Finally, demand

818   characteristics were evaluated by asking participants whether they believe that playing

819   Tetris will decrease intrusive images, using a sliding scale from -10 (extreme

820   decrease) to +10 (extreme increase).

821

822   Diary

823           Participants were sent a questionnaire at the end of each day, on the day of the

824   session and for the three days following the lab-based session. Both verbal and written

825   instructions were given on how to complete the diary. An intrusive memory was

826   defined as “a mental image, [that] may pop into your mind, out of the blue, and not at

827   times when you are deliberately thinking about the film”, in line with the VRT
                                                                                                37


828   involuntary instructions. Participants were told to not include memories of the film that

829   were retrieved deliberately. In the online questionnaire, participants received the

830   following prompts: ‘(i) How many involuntary memories (images/sounds) about the film

831   have popped into your mind today when you were not deliberately thinking about it;

832   and (ii) Please describe what you pictured or heard in these memories to the best of

833   your ability. Even if you have several involuntary memories that are the same moment

834   of the film, please record each individual one. If you don’t have any involuntary

835   memories pertaining to the film, please record this as ‘0’.’ Participants typed their

836   intrusive memory descriptions into the diary in an unconstrained text field. Participants’

837   descriptions of the intrusive memories were used to verify whether the intrusion related

838   to the film or not.

839   Data analysis
840         We counted the total number of button-presses in the first VRT, excluding the

841   3.9% of the reports which could not be linked to any of the film clips (DV1). DV1 was

842   used to test hypotheses A and B. We also counted the number of unique film details

843   reported in the first VRT, excluding repetitions of recurring details (DV2). As is typical

844   with recall of films, “details” were coded according to the Autobiographical Interview

845   (Levine et al., 2002). DV2 was used for Hypotheses A-C. These data naturally include

846   ambiguities, for example, where it is not fully clear that a response refers to any film

847   detail, where it may refer to more than one film detail, or where it seems the participant

848   has perceived a film detail incorrectly (e.g., a pilot participant perceived blood drops

849   as insects). Using the Autobiographical Interview coding scheme (Levine et al., 2002),

850   data processing classified reports as “internal details” (reports that refer to film detail),

851   “external detail” (reports that do not refer to film detail), “errors” (reports that refer to

852   content that was not studied in the experiment) and “other” responses. At least 20%
                                                                                                 38


853   of the datasets, and all external detail, error and other responses, were coded

854   independently by a second rater. Where they disagreed with the first rater, these were

855   discussed by the entire team to reach resolution.

856          DV1 and DV2 were scored using the qualitative analysis software Taguette

857   (https://app.taguette.org). Each press of the space key was tagged with the film clip

858   number it referred to or tagged as an ‘invalid’ press if it did not include any reported

859   memory of the film (3.9% of presses). DV1 was calculated by subtracting total invalid

860   presses from total space key presses. Text within each entry was then tagged for

861   Internal details from the film, repetitions of these Internal details, External details

862   (non-film-related content) or errors, based on the Autobiographical Interview scoring

863   system used for the free recall data analysis in Lau-Zhu et al. (2019). DV2 was

864   calculated using the total number of Internal details from the film, excluding

865   repetitions. Counts of repeated internal details or external details are provided in

866   Supplementary Table 2. The same scoring process was conducted by a second rater

867   for 20% of all data files, to assess inter-reliability of Internal detail scoring (ICC =

868   0.993 for first VRT scoring and ICC = 0.962 for second VRT scoring). Both raters

869   were blinded to the intervention and retrieval conditions.

870          The seven mood rating variables (sad, happy, anxious, depressed, calm,

871   hopeless, angry), each measured on 0 to 100 Likert scales, were averaged into a

872   composite ‘Negative Affect’ score, with positively valenced items (happy and calm)

873   being reverse scored (e.g., a rating of 10 points for ‘happy’ is entered as -10).

874          In the online diary, participants reported the total number of involuntary

875   memories they experienced each day and entered text to describe content of those

876   memories. To ensure that valid, accurate, numeric responses were recorded, the

877   research team verified the memory counts for each day. If none of the written memory
                                                                                            39


878   content was from the film, then the count number was converted to zero (7

879   occurrences). Similarly, if the written content only indicated general film-related

880   thoughts rather than involuntary memories (e.g., “Did not get flashbacks of specific

881   scenes but got an overall sense of sadness all three times”), then the count was

882   converted to zero (1 occurrence). In cases where non-film-related or non-intrusion

883   descriptions were accompanied by other valid entries, one was subtracted from the

884   inputted count (1 occurrence). With these verified counts for each day, a combined

885   total across all days was calculated, with any missing values for a given day (Day 0:

886   n=22, Day 1: n=18, Day 2: n=26, Day 3: n=16) treated as zeros in this addition (rather

887   than using imputation as in previous studies).

893   equipment malfunction, or participant did not watch the film). We pre-registered that

905   Statistical analyses

906          Our first two hypotheses are derived from the DRA, but evidence for the null

907   could support a unitary account of trauma memory, while the opposite is true for

908   Hypothesis D. Because the strength of the evidence for both the experimental

909   hypothesis and the null hypothesis were of interest, we used Bayesian statistics to

910   examine our results. All statistical analyses used the package BayesFactor (Morey &

911   Rouder, 2018).

912          Based on pilot data, we expected DV1 to range between 5-32 key presses and

913   DV2 to range between 8-86, and for both DV1 and DV2 to have an approximate

914   Gaussian distribution, such that the count data can be treated as continuous data and

915   parametric statistics applied. We therefore tested hypotheses A and B with a 2-way

916   ANOVA using the anovaBF function. These hypotheses correspond to different 2X2

917   interactions within this design (see Table 1) and were evaluated against models with

918   main effects only. In the presence of interactions, follow-up tests of simple effects were

919   performed on subsets of the data. Hypothesis C corresponds to the simple effect of

920   emotion in the voluntary/control intervention condition and was examined using a 1-

921   way ANOVA. Hypothesis D was tested with correlationBF from the same R package.

922          That said, it was also possible that count numbers for DV1 and DV2 could be

923   low, skewed, and zero-inflated. In this case, we pre-registered to switch to

924   implementing the same ANOVA contrasts, but within the brm function of the brms

925   package (Bürkner, 2017), which enables various linking functions to handle such count

926   data. Specifically, we planned to replicate the brm models used to handle similar count
                                                                                            41


927   data by Ramineni et al. (2023; see also https://osf.io/m5cvj/), using model comparison

928   to determine the best model, from which we estimated the corresponding Bayes

929   Factors as above. The code we pre-registered to use to apply and compare brm

930   models can be found within the folder ‘BRMS code’ under the following link:

931   https://osf.io/cms7n/files/s2g9h?view_only=0fa9878af33b40de9a54581c71af5217.

932   When fitting brms models in the reported analyses, diffuse normal priors N(0,10) were

933   initially employed for all regression coefficients, based on the approach employed in

934   Ramineni et al. (2023). As sensitivity analyses, more constrained normal priors N(0,1)

935   were also implemented for all regression coefficients.

936          The following analyses were conducted for the purpose of manipulation checks:

937   (i) a numerical comparison between the number of diary intrusions and involuntary

938   memory reports from the version of the VRT used here and the earlier version of the

939   VRT in previous studies to indicate the extent to which our sample was similar in their

940   response in the trauma condition (ii) a comparison between the baseline mood ratings

941   in the trauma vs. the neutral groups, and (iii) a comparison between pre- and post-film

942   mood rating in the trauma vs. the neutral groups, to ensure that the neutral film did not

943   induce negative mood in participants.

944          We planned to conduct a number of secondary analyses to examine additional

945   questions, all using functions from the BayesFactor package. Although our diary

946   measure was taken only over the course of 4 days (once daily), and uses an electronic

947   method, we believed that it would still be of interest to use the number of intrusions

948   reported in the diary as DV to examine Hypothesis B, and to validate the VRT by

949   examining whether VRT and diary measures of involuntary memories are correlated

950   across participants.

1144                                         Discussion
1145          The diary reports in participants who took part in the negative condition indicate

1146   that the trauma film successfully induced intrusive memories. In the negative condition,

1147   the number of intrusions reported during the involuntary VRT was positively correlated

1148   with the number of intrusions reported in the diary, supporting the VRT as a laboratory

1149   operationalisation of conditions which give rise to involuntary memory in the real world.

1150   The self-reported mood data also shows that the manipulation of emotion in this

1151   experiment was successful. This is evident in that compared to the neutral group, the

1152   negative group reported more negative affect after the film, rated the film as more

1153   distressing, and rated the individual clips from the film as more impactful, negative,

1154   and arousing. Despite these successful manipulation checks, none of our predictions

1155   that were derived from the DRA were supported

1156          Our experimental design had assumed that visuospatial intervention would

1157   interfere with involuntary memory in the negative film condition. This assumption was

1158   based on the balance of evidence that was reviewed above and supported by recent

1159   meta-analytic evidence (Varma et al., 2024, but see exceptions mentioned previously

1160   with null and mixed findings). Indeed, the memory dissociation findings of Lau-Zhu et

1161   al. (2019, 2021) was the impetus for the research presented here and the reason we

1162   hypothesized that this visuospatial intervention would decrease involuntary memory

1163   more than voluntary memory (Hypothesis A) and negative involuntary memory more

1164   than neutral involuntary memory (Hypothesis B). However, the comparison between

1165   the visuospatial interference intervention and the control intervention in the negative
                                                                                              52


1166   film condition indicated that there was moderate evidence for a null interference effect.

1167   This finding decreases confidence in the claim that voluntary and involuntary memory

1168   can be functionally dissociated. We discuss the implications of these results in the

1169   General Discussion.

1170          Our data collection process was blinded, so it was only when data collection

1171   was terminated and we viewed the data for the first time that we realised that the

1172   visuospatial intervention failed to decrease involuntary memories in the negative

1173   condition. In hindsight, we should have included a test for the presence of this effect

1174   in our stopping rule, as a condition of continued data collection. Because in this dataset

1175   we did not observe a selective interference effect, Hypotheses A and B, which defined

1176   the stopping rule, were not supported. This was despite the fact that we conducted

1177   some pilot testing so trainee researchers could get feedback and monitor how

1178   participants responded, although experimenter training here was less detailed as in

1179   Lau-Zhu et al. (2019). We reviewed experimenter training (see McConnell et al., 2026)

1180   and enhanced it in Experiment 2.

1181          The unexpected null effect of the visuospatial intervention does not impinge on

1182   Hypothesis C, which like the others, was also derived from the DRA. Hypothesis C

1183   was conditionalized on finding diary reports of involuntary memory in the negative

1184   condition, because according to the DRA, the presence of intrusions suggests that

1185   some quasi-pathological encoding has occurred, at least at the level of the group.

1186   Having satisfied this condition, we next tested the prediction derived from this theory

1187   that voluntary memory for negative films will be impaired. The results, tested only in

1188   participants who conducted the voluntary memory test first, contradicted this

1189   hypothesis, with substantial evidence that voluntary memory for negative films did not

1190   differ in the number of details from that of neutral films.
                                                                                            53


1191         While the finding that participants in the negative condition, who reported a rise

1192   in negative affect and emotional distress, did not recall more than participants in the

1193   neutral condition (in either voluntary or involuntary measures) may initially appear

1194   inconsistent with prior findings of emotionally enhanced memory, it aligns with

1195   predictions of unitary accounts of memory. In our previous publications, we have

1196   frequently observed an emotional list composition effect: that emotion increases

1197   memory when emotional and neutral items are intermixed at study and test, but not

1198   when such items are blocked, i.e., when emotional stimuli are studied and recalled

1199   separately from neutral stimuli (Talmi, Luk et al., 2007; Talmi & McGarry, 2012;

1200   Barnacle et al., 2017). This effect was observed by other groups (Hadley & MacKay,

1201   2006; Garcia, Ritchey & Kensinger, 2023), although failed replications have also been

1202   reported, where emotion enhanced memory regardless of list composition (Zarubin et

1203   al., 2020; Baraly et al., 2019). eCMR and CMR3 are unitary models of memory that

1204   account for the emotional list composition effect (Talmi et al., 2019; Cohen & Kahana,

1205   2022). They suggest that although emotional experiences always draw processing

1206   resources and therefore have a ‘stronger’ memory trace (which in the models is

1207   operationalised as an increased learning rate), this only gives them a mnemonic

1208   advantage when the memory test that takes place at test includes weaker items, those

1209   being neutral experiences in mixed list conditions.

1210         Given the balance of evidence that underlies the effects of visuospatial

1211   interventions to reduce intrusive memories both in the laboratory (Asselbergs et al.,

1212   2024; Badawi et al., 2020; Deeprose et al., 2012; Holmes et al., 2009, 2010; James et

1213   al., 2015; Kessler et al., 2020; Lau-Zhu et al., 2019, 2021) and in the clinic (e.g.,

1214   Iyadurai et al., 2018; Kanstrup et al., 2024), the null effect of the visuospatial

1215   intervention here is more similar to other studies that have either found no reduction
                                                                                             54


1216   in intrusive memories (e.g., Asselbergs et al., 2018; Brühl et al., 2019; Brennen et al.,

1217   2021; Broughill et al., 2025; Matura et al., 2025), or only on immediate but not longer-

1218   term measures (Wessel et al., 2025). It is natural to wonder what aspect of the protocol

1219   of Experiment 1 resulted in the failure to replicate some of the previous studies.

1220   Experiment 2 is the result of extensive discussions and represents our best attempt to

1221   replicate the previously observed effects of visuospatial interference on lab-based

1222   intrusions using a refined methodological approach closer to Lau Zhu et al. (2019).


1223                                      Experiment 2
1224          Experiment 2 was designed to give us the best chance of replicating previous

1225   findings by Lau-Zhu et al. (2019, Experiment 2) that a visuospatial intervention

1226   decreases involuntary memories within the trauma film paradigm. For this purpose,

1227   we focused on two of the eight conditions employed in Experiment 1, comparing only

1228   two groups, both of which encoded only the negative film clips from Experiment 1. One

1229   group received a visuospatial intervention, and the other served as a control group.

1230   We retained the same dependent measures from Experiment 1, i.e. the involuntary

1231   VRT and diary, but did not include the voluntary VRT.

1232          The protocol of Experiment 2 further differed from the protocol of Experiment 1

1233   in the following ways. First, the verbal control task employed in Experiment 1 (listening

1234   to a political podcast) was replaced with a passive rest period, to more closely align

1235   with Lau-Zhu et al. (2019). This change addressed the possibility that our previous

1236   control task was not sufficiently neutral in topic and may have unintentionally elicited

1237   imaginal cognition. Second, while in Experiment 1 the Visuospatial task instructions

1238   and practice took place after the reminder cues, all participants in Experiment 2

1239   completed a practice session of Tetris with instructions emphasising mental rotation,

1240   before film viewing. This reduces the interval between delivering reminder cues and
                                                                                             55


1241   beginning the intervention and more directly aligned with the protocol used in Lau-Zhu

1242   et al. (2019). Third, the structured filler period between the film and the reminder cues

1243   was increased from 5 min in Experiment 1 to 30 min in Experiment 2, replicating the

1244   30 min filler period in Lau-Zhu et al. (2019).

1245          Additionally, in Experiment 2, the experimenter was fully blinded to participants'

1246   allocation to the intervention or control condition, with post-reminder task instructions

1247   administered by a computer program without the experimenter present. This is in

1248   contrast with both the present Experiment 1 and Lau-Zhu et al. (2019), in which the

1249   experimenter directly administered the intervention, creating the potential for

1250   experimenter-related bias in subsequent memory measures. Supplementary Table 1

1251   lists the remaining procedural similarities and differences between the current

1252   Experiment 2 and Experiment 2 of Lau-Zhu et al. (2019).

1253          Finally, we reflected on the training experimenters typically receive in

1254   experimental psychopathology research and considered good practice (McConnell et

1255   al., 2026). Unlike Experiment 1, which involved only a brief period of experimenter

1256   training, more extensive experimenter training was employed prior to Experiment 2,

1257   across several expert-guided sessions online and one in person, covering the delivery

1258   of trauma film viewing instructions, mental rotation instructions and involuntary

1259   memory measurement instructions.

1275   Participants
1276          We employed a Bayesian sequential design (Schönbrodt & Wagenmakers,

1277   2018), beginning with an initial sample of n = 20 per group and adding data in

1278   batches of 10 per group. Data collection continued until reaching strong evidence for

1279   the alternative hypothesis (BF₁₀ > 6) or the null hypothesis (BF₀₁ > 6), or at a

1280   maximum of n = 60 per group. The stopping decision was based only on the number

1281   of memories reported in the Involuntary VRT (DV1).

1282          A large effect size was expected, based on previous research cited above

1283   (e.g., Lau-Zhu et al., 2019, Experiment 2 VRT: d = 0.96). To evaluate our sequential

1284   design, Monte Carlo simulations were conducted in R. In each of 5000 iterations,

1285   data for two independent groups were sampled in batches of 10, starting from a

1286   minimum of n = 20 per group and ending either when the Bayes Factor reached the

1287   predefined decision thresholds or when a maximum of n = 60 was reached. Bayes

1288   factors were calculated using the ttestBF function from the BayesFactor package

1289   (Morey & Rouder, 2018), with default priors and a one-tailed null interval, reflecting

1290   the directional hypothesis that DV1 will be lower in the Visuospatial group than the

1291   Control group.
                                                                                                57


1292          Under a simulated large effect size (d = 0.80), the median final sample size

1293   was the minimum of 20 per group, with 97.72% of simulations reaching BF₁₀ > 6,

1294   while only 2.28% reached the maximum sample size without conclusive evidence at

1295   BF₁₀ > 6 or BF₀₁ > 6. The rate of incorrect conclusions in favour of the null (BF₀₁ > 6)

1296   was 0.00%, indicating negligible risk of false negatives in the case of a true effect. To

1297   assess the potential to detect a true null effect, simulations were also run with the

1298   effect size set to zero (d = 0.00). In these null simulations, the median final sample

1299   size was the maximum 60 per group, with 51.00% of simulations producing strong

1300   evidence for the null (BF₀₁ > 6), while 46.58% were inconclusive at BF₁₀ > 6 or

1301   BF₀₁ > 6 and only 2.42% incorrectly supported the alternative (BF₁₀ > 6). This

1302   demonstrates that our design had potential to support the null hypothesis if true, with

1303   minimal scope for false positives.

1304          To minimise any potential bias during data analysis, at each stopping point,

1305   the personnel who conducted the initial data analysis utilised an automated analysis

1306   script, which provided them only with a ‘stop’ or ‘go’ answer, but not the BF

1307   associated with the hypothesis (Sadibolova & Terhune, 2025).

1308          As in Experiment 1, participants were recruited from the local community

1309   through the Cambridge SONA platform. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were the

1310   same as Experiment 1.

1311          The automated analysis script did not indicate stopping at any of the

1312   preregistered stopping points. Therefore, testing continued to the maximum sample

1313   size of N=120. Data exclusions followed the pre-registered plan. During the course of

1314   testing, three participants were replaced due to technical issues during data

1315   collection. Upon reaching the maximum preregistered sample size outlier checks

1316   were conducted and 8 participants were replaced whose performance data were
                                                                                                58


1317   outliers with respect to their group. The final sample included 60 participants in the

1318   Visuospatial group (44 females and 16 males; age M = 29.98, SD= 13.94) and 60 in

1319   the Control group (40 females and 20 males; age M = 28.75, SD = 12.03).

1440                                         Discussion

1441          While the principal aim of Experiment 2 was to replicate previous findings that

1442   a visuospatial intervention decreases involuntary memories of analogue trauma

1443   (Asselbergs et al., 2024; Deeprose et al., 2012; Holmes et al., 2009, 2010; James et

1444   al., 2015; Kessler et al., 2020; Lau-Zhu et al., 2019, 2021; see also Varma et al.,

1445   2024 for a meta-analysis featuring 11 experiments using this intervention, and other

1446   null findings mentioned above), the results instead reaffirmed those of Experiment 1.

1447   The negative film once again elicited a clear rise in negative affect, as well as

1448   comparable levels of lab- and diary-based involuntary memories to previous trauma

1449   film research. Validating the vigilance-retrieval task we employed, intrusions within

1450   this lab-based task were again predictive of subsequent diary intrusions. Yet, despite

1451   extensive efforts to match the methodology of this experiment to that of previous

1452   work (Lau-Zhu., 2019, see Supplementary Table 1), including by using a passive
                                                                                               65


1453   rather than active control condition, we still observed no evidence that visuospatial

1454   intervention decreased involuntary memories. Tests of all three dependent measures

1455   (DV1, DV2 and Diary Total) supported the null hypothesis.

1456          The Bayesian sequential design, which reached the maximum planned

1457   sample size of N=120, ensures this non-replication is extremely unlikely to be

1458   attributable to Type II error. Confidence in the present findings is further bolstered by

1459   the use of experimenter-blinded allocation to the intervention and rest conditions, a

1460   form of experimental control absent from the experiments of Lau-Zhu et al. (2019).

1461          The impetus for Experiment 2 had been to produce an interference effect on

1462   lab-based involuntary memory of a negative film, in order to subsequently test our

1463   initial DRA-derived hypotheses regarding the selectivity of such interference (i.e.,

1464   whether the interference would extend to voluntary memory and to neutral memory).

1465   However, our results suggest this is not a feasible approach, at least in the context of

1466   an involuntary vigilance-retrieval task administered within the same lab session as

1467   film encoding and the visuospatial intervention. In the General Discussion below, we

1468   consider the wider implications of these findings.


1469                                  General Discussion

1470          The present research was motivated by intriguing evidence that involuntary

1471   and voluntary memory could be functionally dissociated. In two studies led by Lau-

1472   Zhu and two of the present co-authors (Lau-Zhu et al., 2019, 2021), involuntary

1473   memory of a trauma film was decreased in the group who had undergone a

1474   visuospatial intervention (film reminder cues followed by engaging in a visuospatial

1475   task), while voluntary memory for the same material was not affected. These studies

1476   were conducted to follow up on conceptually similar findings, where similar
                                                                                               66


1477   visuospatial interventions decreased diary-reported intrusions but not voluntary

1478   memory for the same material (Asselbergs et al., 2024; Deeprose et al., 2012;

1479   Holmes et al., 2009; James et al., 2015). Collectively, such results supported the

1480   DRA, a theory that challenged unitary accounts of episodic memory, such as the

1481   eCMR developed by another present co-author (Talmi et al., 2019). We formed an

1482   adversarial collaboration to explore this issue together, adopting state-of-the-art,

1483   resource-intensive, stringently controlled methodology, which involved in-person

1484   testing of over 400 research volunteers.

1485          Experiment 1 was carefully designed to test four hypotheses derived from the

1486   DRA, and sharpened through extensive discussion with reviewers on the PCI panel

1487   (https://rr.peercommunityin.org/articles/rec?id=807). The data did not support any of

1488   these hypotheses. The failure to support Hypotheses A and B stemmed directly from

1489   the failure to replicate the reported effect of a visuospatial intervention on involuntary

1490   memory. In Experiment 2, we refined our methodology but again failed to replicate

1491   the expected interference effect. Given the robust evidence that participants in these

1492   experiments experienced intrusions, proponents of the DRA would suggest that the

1493   trauma film encoding was quasi-pathological (Brewin & Field, 2024). In this situation,

1494   the DRA predicts further that voluntary memory for the trauma film would be

1495   impaired relative to memory of a neutral film (Hypothesis C), but we failed to support

1496   that hypothesis as well. Similarly, the DRA would suggest that involuntary and

1497   voluntary memory of a trauma film should be uncorrelated because they rely on

1498   separate memory traces (Hypothesis D); yet we found clear evidence against this

1499   prediction, with the two forms of recall being highly positively correlated. Taken

1500   together, our results appear to challenge the DRA. The similarity between the effects
                                                                                              67


1501   of experimental manipulations on voluntary and involuntary memory instead aligns

1502   more closely with unitary accounts of episodic memory.

1503          In the discussion to follow, we will consider in greater depth the context,

1504   implications and limitations of each of these findings, before engaging in a broader

1505   evaluation of both the DRA and other contemporary theoretical accounts of traumatic

1506   memory processes, informed by the present findings.

1507

1508   Hypotheses A and B: Visuospatial Interference Did Not Impact Subsequent

1509   Involuntary or Voluntary Memory of an Analogue Trauma or Neutral Film

1510          While the present study aimed to test the selectivity of the visuospatial

1511   interference effect, it instead produced consistent evidence against an effect of

1512   visuospatial interference on any of the forms of memory measured here. The

1513   absence of effects on voluntary memory was expected based on similar findings

1514   across 11 previous experiments (Asselbergs et al., 2024; Deeprose et al., 2012,

1515   Experiment 1; Holmes et al., 2009; Holmes et al., 2010, Experiments 1 and 2; James

1516   et al., 2015, Experiments 1 and 2; Kessler et al., 2020; Lau-Zhu et al., 2019,

1517   Experiments 1 and 2; Lau-Zhu et al., 2021), but when we formed the null hypothesis

1518   for a main effect of the visuospatial intervention, we considered that matching the

1519   voluntary and involuntary conditions more tightly here by measuring both through the

1520   VRT could result in an effect of the visuospatial intervention on the voluntary

1521   measure as well. This possibility had some precedent in the literature, as unlike the

1522   aforementioned studies, Hemi et al. (2023) had found that visuospatial interference

1523   led to both reduced intrusive memories and lower scores on recognition memory of a

1524   trauma film. By contrast, the absence of effects on not only voluntary, but also
                                                                                                  68


1525   involuntary memories here, across our two large-scale experiments, was not

1526   predicted.

1527          Considering the diary measure, the majority of lab-based evidence employing

1528   a visuospatial intervention has found effects on diary reports of involuntary memories

1529   in the 7 days following the exposure to trauma analogue material (see Varma et al.,

1530   2024, for a meta-analysis of 11 experiments), but several studies have not

1531   (Asselbergs et al., 2018; Brühl et al., 2019; Brennen et al., 2021; Broughill et al.,

1532   2025), with one only finding intrusion reduction effects on short-term but not longer-

1533   term measures of intrusive memories (Wessel et al., 2025) and another only finding

1534   intrusion reduction effects with one visuospatial task but not a different visuospatial

1535   task (Matura et al., 2025). In some cases, null results might be attributed to

1536   observable deviations from the original protocol (i.e., Holmes et al., 2009), such as

1537   use of a visuospatial task that differed in critical ways (Asselbergs et al., 2018), the

1538   omission of film reminder cues prior to the visuospatial task (Brühl et al., 2019), or

1539   the use of a visual vigilance task as the control intervention, rather than using a

1540   modality different from the visuospatial interference task (Wessel et al., 2025). In

1541   other cases, it is less clear, and one can only speculate about confounders. For

1542   example, it might be that providing a sizeable payment before (rather than after)

1543   completing the diary leads to poor diary adherence (Brennen et al., 2021), that

1544   unsupervised administration of the intervention online is far less reliable than in-

1545   person (Broughill et al., 2025), or that including a structured break itself decreases

1546   intrusions (Hørlyck et al., 2019). Our Bayesian evidence against an intervention

1547   effect in the diary in both of the present experiments is, however, less likely to be

1548   attributable to the above methodological deviations, particularly in the context of our

1549   second experiment, which was designed to closely match Lau-Zhu et al. (2019).
                                                                                                69


1550   Nonetheless, subtle but potentially important methodological differences between

1551   that experiment and present work remained, as discussed below.

1552          Even if the lack of an intervention effect on diary intrusions in the present

1553   experiments might owe to methodological differences in the diary, the lack of an

1554   intervention effect on VRT intrusions is harder to explain. Such VRT intervention

1555   effects have been reported in three previous experiments (Lau-Zhu et al., 2019,

1556   Experiments 2 & 3; Lau-Zhu et al., 2021), which we aimed to replicate closely here in

1557   two studies with greater statistical power (per group n = 30 or 60 vs per group n = 18

1558   or 19). Aligning with the present findings, recent research from deBettencourt et al.

1559   (2026) using an AI-guided implementation of this visuospatial intervention similarly

1560   found that, relative to a podcast control condition, the intervention had no effect on

1561   intrusions within a same-day involuntary VRT, despite reducing intrusions in a

1562   subsequent diary measure. Notably, their version of the VRT did not require memory

1563   reports, suggesting that the intervention is unlikely to decrease VRT intrusions

1564   regardless of the extent of reporting requirements. Taken together, these data

1565   provide compelling evidence that, despite initially promising findings from Lau-Zhu

1566   and colleagues, a same-day involuntary VRT does not show effects of visuospatial

1567   interference.

1568          Considered more broadly, the present findings evidently call into question the

1569   replicability and possible boundary conditions of lab-based research employing

1570   visuospatial interventions in the context of analogue trauma in healthy participants

1571   and underscore that effects may be fragile and hard to replicate. We now consider

1572   possible boundary effects and speculate on their potential implications on diary

1573   measures in this study, noting the limitation that the following suggestions are post-

1574   hoc. First, since the diary here was not our main focus and to reduce burden, it was
                                                                                                  70


1575   briefer (across four days with one prompt a day, rather than across seven days with

1576   multiple prompts per day) and administered online rather than pen-and-paper, which

1577   might have reduced sensitivity. However, most intrusions occur in the first days after

1578   watching the film and we recorded a similar trajectory of intrusion frequency as in

1579   previous studies (e.g., James et al., 2015). Second, Lau-Zhu and colleagues

1580   administered a single VRT between the intervention and the diary instructions, while

1581   in Experiment 1 we administered two. This took approximately 40 minutes, 3.5 times

1582   longer than the intervention itself. This is unlikely to be the cause of the null

1583   intervention effect, since a recognition task of similar length was administered in Lau-

1584   Zhu et al. (2021) and Experiment 2 replicated the null effect although only a single

1585   VRT was administered.

1586          A more likely explanation is that the effect of the visuospatial intervention on

1587   the diary is found only when the intervention is not followed by detailed memory

1588   reporting that day. Krans and colleagues have shown that administering a verbal

1589   memory measure post-film (a recognition test for just one part of the film) reduced

1590   intrusions for that part of the film (Krans et al., 2009). This test contained neutrally

1591   formulated items intended to rehearse information from the film in the form of

1592   statements that participants had to verify as true or false, such as “There were three

1593   doctors in white coats present at the scene”. The authors suggested that by

1594   performing this memory test immediately after the trauma film, and providing a

1595   memory rehearsal opportunity, these parts of the memory became more integrated,

1596   less isolated and less fragmented, and therefore less likely to intrude. Although

1597   memory reporting requirements do not always have this effect (Lau-Zhu et al., 2019,

1598   2021), Krans et al.’s results suggest that reflecting on trauma-analogue memories

1599   within the VRT, may have inadvertently overridden the attenuating effect of the
                                                                                                 71


1600   intervention task in the experiments reported here, obfuscating differences between

1601   conditions in the intrusion diary.

1602          While the experiments which inspired our collaborative research (Lau-Zhu et

1603   al., 2019, 2021) each observed effects of the visuospatial intervention on diary

1604   intrusions, it is possible that apparently subtle differences in the methodology,

1605   described in Supplementary Table 1 and Appendix, caused our participants to

1606   provide more detailed memory reports, which attenuated the effects of the

1607   visuospatial intervention. We speculate that the unconstrained box which captured

1608   memory reports, and the ability to type into it (rather than write with a pen) could

1609   have played a role. Additionally, Lau-Zhu and colleagues told participants to “briefly

1610   describe the content of the image” so that they could “check it is related to the film”

1611   (e.g., the clip of an eye surgery versus a rampaging elephant), while we instructed

1612   participants instead to “provide a brief description that allows us to understand what

1613   you saw in your mind’s eye”. While these statements are similar, the changes we

1614   made nevertheless could have altered how participants approached this task.

1615   Although we do not have data to support this claim that the Lau-Zhu et al. (2019)

1616   Experiment 2 descriptions were shorter, in that study memory reports were written in

1617   a small box using pen-and-paper, and likely to have been brief, amounting to just a

1618   few words. By contrast, the unconstrained text entry employed here allowed for

1619   memory reports of widely varying lengths (for illustrative purposes, “the image of the

1620   elephant terrorizing people came to mind. It was trampling everything in its path. It

1621   was probably scared and confused”, “elephant”, and “an elephant escaped the

1622   circus”). In the Involuntary VRT, text entry length following each memory report key

1623   press ranged from 1 to 83 words (M = 9.37, SD = 7.46) in Experiment 1 and from 1

1624   to 75 words (M = 8.11, SD = 6.69) in Experiment 2. This speaks to the possibility that
                                                                                                  72


1625   elaboration upon involuntary memories in this task has obfuscated a potential

1626   intervention effect in the subsequent diary measure.

1627          Supporting this possibility, in an experiment focused on clinical analogues of

1628   the reduction of intrusive memories through an intervention, rather than testing

1629   models of memory per se, no intrusion descriptions in the VRT were allowed

1630   (deBettencourt et al., 2026). Critically, in line with the possibility that memory

1631   elaboration, either in a VRT (here) or in voluntary tests (Krans et al., 2009) may

1632   impact intrusions, deBettencourt and colleagues observed decreased intrusions in

1633   the diary. Our results thus support the decision, in recent clinical studies, not to

1634   prompt intrusive memory descriptions in the daily diaries but rather focus only on

1635   intrusion frequency (Ramineni et al., 2023; Beckenstrom et al., 2026).That said,

1636   whether the interference effect deBettencourt et al. (2026) observed in the diary is

1637   attributable to differences in the implementation of the diary measure (e.g., 7 day

1638   duration, rather than 4 here), the use of a more targeted reactivation procedure

1639   before the visuospatial task (guided recall of subjective ‘worst moments’ of film,

1640   rather than the standardised film reminder cue images employed here), or

1641   eliminating memory reporting in the VRT requires additional research. Clearly, such

1642   a constraint on involuntary memory reporting does, in the context of experimental

1643   research, limit the potential to test for selective interference between involuntary and

1644   voluntary memory under controlled laboratory conditions.

1645          In this context, it is natural to consider other means of empirically testing the

1646   DRA in this sample. With this in mind, we now discuss the present findings with

1647   respect to two distinct predictions derived from the DRA, which did not depend upon

1648   the presence of a visuospatial interference effect.

1650   Hypothesis C: Voluntary Recall was Unimpaired by an Analogue Trauma

1651          The present Experiment 1 provided no indication that voluntary memory was

1652   reduced in the negative condition relative to the neutral condition, contrary to

1653   Hypothesis C. Before considering the implications of this finding, we briefly return to

1654   the method by which we derived this hypothesis from the DRA.

1655          Early iterations of the DRA (Brewin et al., 2010) argued that voluntary recall

1656   may be impaired in PTSD only for the most intense moments of a traumatic

1657   experience: “it may be only certain traumatic moments (hotspots) that are missing

1658   from the C-rep". Moreover, these initial accounts argued that mild emotional

1659   manipulations (such as those experienced in lab-based studies of emotional

1660   memory) may actually enhance rather than impair voluntary memory encoding.

1661   However, these accounts agree that C-reps in the hippocampus will be impaired

1662   when this material is encoded in a pathological way, something that should impact

1663   adversely any memory measure that relies on intact hippocampal associations, such

1664   as free recall. Crucially, the DRA suggests that the presence of intrusions could

1665   indicate that pathological encoding occurred. We designed the study to enable

1666   detection of pathological encoding by employing an experimental analogue of

1667   trauma, which has induced involuntary memories in previous work (e.g., James et

1668   al., 2016; Lau-Zhu et al., 2019, 2021) and in the present research. Our finding of

1669   unimpaired recall of trauma-analogue material in a sample which presented with

1670   substantial involuntary memory for the same material therefore directly contradicts

1671   these early iterations of the DRA.

1672          Subsequent iterations of the DRA (Bisby et al., 2016, 2018, 2020), based on

1673   neuroimaging data in healthy participants, extended its explanatory scope.

1674   Specifically, these later extensions of the DRA predicted that even relatively mild
                                                                                               74


1675   emotional manipulations would down-regulate hippocampal function (Bisby et al.,

1676   2016, 2018, 2020). Initial empirical findings supported this prediction: the

1677   researchers observed decreased BOLD signal in the hippocampus when healthy

1678   participants encoded emotional materials, and behaviourally, such material impaired

1679   associative binding, along with reducing coherence of voluntary recall (i.e., impaired

1680   C-reps) and enhancing memory of individual sensory elements (i.e., enhanced S-

1681   reps).

1682            The present finding of unimpaired voluntary recall under analogue trauma

1683   appears to directly contradict this latter conceptualisation of the DRA. Instead, the

1684   present finding aligns more closely with other research (Bogdan et al., 2024; de

1685   Montpellier et al., 2025), where negative emotion did not impact paired-associate

1686   learning when attentional differences at encoding were controlled. Thus, these

1687   findings taken together provide compelling counterevidence to the argument that

1688   negative emotion impairs voluntary memory encoding via hippocampal down-

1689   regulation (Bisby et al., 2016, 2018, 2020). Furthermore, Kobelt et al. (2024), who

1690   examined neural activity during the encoding of a trauma film, found that the

1691   encoding of materials which subsequently became intrusive was associated with

1692   increased, rather than decreased, hippocampal activation, the extent of which

1693   positively correlated with amygdala activation. Additionally, while hippocampal

1694   activation was decreased during encoding of negative emotional material in Bisby et

1695   al. (2018), the nature of the material did not influence subsequent memory effects in

1696   the hippocampus in that study, and in Madan et al. (2017). Hence, there is

1697   accumulating behavioural and neural evidence that negative emotion impairs neither

1698   hippocampal function during memory encoding nor resultant voluntary recall, even in

1699   the most direct laboratory analogue of traumatic memory.
                                                                                               75


1700            The inverse prediction, made in some formulations of the DRA - that the

1701   “perception of a moderately stressful or emotionally salient event results in the

1702   creation of more enduring C-reps” (Brewin et al., 2010, p. 221) - is also not

1703   supported by the present data, as there was equally no credible Bayesian evidence

1704   of enhanced recall of the trauma film relative to the neutral film. Despite using the

1705   most intense emotional material that is ethically permissible, we did not observe

1706   emotionally enhanced memory. As noted in the Discussion section of Experiment 1,

1707   this absence of an effect of emotion on recall was predicted by a unitary

1708   computational account of emotional memory (Talmi et al., 2019). According to this

1709   account, emotionally enhanced memory is due to competition between emotional

1710   and neutral stimuli during retrieval, and therefore not typically observed in between-

1711   list (here, between-participant) designs when stimuli are matched on semantic

1712   similarity (Ack-Baraly et al., 2016; Talmi, 2013).

1713            To summarise then, the present data provided no evidence that an analogue

1714   trauma either impairs or enhances the encoding of (hippocampal-dependent)

1715   voluntary memory, as reflected by the number of unique details recalled in the

1716   voluntary VRT. This finding not only contradicts the claims of recent extensions of

1717   the DRA but provides support for unitary alternatives.

1718            Nonetheless, other approaches to quantifying voluntary recall may have

1719   yielded different results. Indeed, future research may consider examining the

1720   coherence of voluntary recall and/or the quantity of spatiotemporal vs. item-level

1721   details recalled, given that Bisby and colleagues point to these as key aspects of

1722   voluntary memory which negative emotion may impair (Bisby et al., 2016, 2018,

1723   2020).

1725   Hypothesis D: Involuntary Recall Predicted Voluntary Recall

1726          By measuring voluntary memory immediately after measuring involuntary

1727   memory, using a matched testing format, Experiment 1 also allowed us to examine

1728   more carefully whether these two forms of memory retrieval derive from separate

1729   traces rather than a single, shared episodic memory representation. Whereas the

1730   DRA would be consistent with a lack of correlation, we found strong positive within-

1731   subject correlations between involuntary and voluntary tests, across both DV1 (i.e.,

1732   the total number of memories) and DV2 (i.e., the number of unique details within

1733   these memories).

1734          As reviewed in Brewin (2014, Table 3), the majority of previous trauma film

1735   paradigm studies seeking to evaluate the correlation between voluntary and

1736   involuntary memory measures have not detected an association between them (e.g.,

1737   Holmes et al., 2004). While clinicians are sensitive to the burden on patients, which

1738   is related to the number of intrusions they experience, even if the content of those

1739   intrusions is the same every time, mainstream memory researchers quantify

1740   voluntary memory through the number of unique items that participants remember.

1741   This leads to a divergence between the two research traditions in the measures they

1742   employ. We have reviewed extensively Lau-Zhu et al. (2019, Experiment 2) where

1743   voluntary and involuntary memory were measured using DV2 and DV1, respectively;

1744   more typically, voluntary memory is measured using a recognition memory test,

1745   where the hit rate is a count of the number of studied items that participants

1746   recognise as “old” (e.g., Hørlyck et al., 2019, Experiment 1; Lau-Zhu et al., 2021). It

1747   is possible, therefore, that previous research may have failed to identify the

1748   associations observed here due to unaddressed differences in the measurement of

1749   involuntary and voluntary memory, both with respect to differing testing formats
                                                                                                77


1750   (diary vs recognition or recall) and differing dependent variables being examined

1751   (counts vs details). Indeed, even measures of voluntary memory are often

1752   “stochastically independent” because of these processing differences, which is why

1753   such findings are considered only limited evidence for the presence of separate

1754   memory systems (Schacter, Wagner & Buckner, 2000).

1755          Some previous research has also observed positive correlations between

1756   measures of involuntary and voluntary memory of a trauma film (Ferree & Cahill,

1757   2009; Ferree et al., 2011). Importantly however, these studies shared two

1758   methodological weaknesses: firstly, they measured intrusions through retrospective

1759   estimates of intrusion frequency rather than direct reporting of each intrusion, and

1760   secondly, this measure of intrusions was delivered after a voluntary memory test. As

1761   Brewin (2014) reasonably argues, this means that intrusion estimates may be biased

1762   by the content of voluntary recall which immediately preceded them. By contrast, our

1763   experimental design mitigates these issues, and provides the most compelling

1764   evidence to date that these two forms of recall are indeed positively associated, a

1765   finding best aligned with unitary accounts (Berntsen, 2021; Cohen & Kahana, 2022;

1766   Rubin et al., 2008; Talmi et al., 2019). That said, a positive correlation could be an

1767   artefact of other individual differences, such as greater motivation in some

1768   participants than others, which would lead them to engage more with the film and

1769   focus more on both retrieval tasks.

1770

1771   Theoretical Implications

1772          The comprehensive lack of support for the DRA in the present study raises

1773   important questions both regarding the generalisability of this account and the

1774   potential promise of unitary alternatives. We found evidence against selective
                                                                                              78


1775   interference, emotion-induced voluntary memory impairment, and uncorrelated

1776   involuntary and voluntary memory traces. Collectively, our findings raise

1777   considerable doubt regarding pre-existing evidence for the DRA derived from

1778   laboratory studies in healthy participants. Based on this collection of findings, we

1779   argue that the DRA has been overextended, from its initial status as a theoretical

1780   account of clinical PTSD (Brewin et al., 1996, 2010; Brewin, 2014), to a wide-

1781   reaching account of emotional memory processes (Bisby et al., 2016, 2018, 2020;

1782   Hørlyck et al., 2019), evoked, for example, to explain phenomena such as the effect

1783   of emotional images on temporal memory (reviewed in Talmi & Palombo, 2025). Our

1784   results do not bear on the question of whether the DRA accounts for the clinical

1785   condition of PTSD, which it was originally conceptualised to explain (Brewin et al.,

1786   1996, 2010; Brewin, 2014).

1787          There are clear implications of the present study with respect to future work

1788   seeking to experimentally evaluate the DRA via investigation of selective

1789   interference. While potential boundary conditions, discussed above, suggest caution

1790   in interpreting the null effect of selective visuospatial interference on the diary

1791   measures, the balance of evidence indicates that such an effect in healthy

1792   participants is not reliable using a same-day lab-based measure of involuntary

1793   memory (VRT). We recommend, therefore, that future research on the selective

1794   interference effect returns to real-life diaries to measure involuntary memory. It is far

1795   harder to control diary reports of involuntary memory so that they are fully

1796   comparable to voluntary memory measures, rendering a stringent test of the DRA

1797   more challenging.

1798          Our research has addressed involuntary memories but not dissociative

1799   flashbacks, which are difficult, if not impossible, to study in healthy samples.
                                                                                              79


1800   Findings from contemporary clinical neuroscience suggest that dissociative

1801   flashbacks in PTSD may rely on distinct, extra-hippocampal, forms of memory

1802   representation (Kearney & Lanius, 2024), perhaps a form of conditioned response

1803   (Milton, 2019).

1804          We hope that the research reported here will encourage investigations that

1805   test predictions of formal unitary models for highly emotionally intense experiences

1806   that are not traumatic. Such work can help develop those unitary models that

1807   followed the tradition of the Temporal Context Model (Howard & Kahana, 2002), and

1808   address limitations that challenge their application to the recall of such experiences.

1809   At present, these models do not account well for the role that the intention to

1810   remember plays in the retrieval process, so they may struggle to account for any

1811   differences between voluntary and involuntary memory (Berntsen, 2023).

1812   Additionally, they do not offer a ready mechanism to explain the influence of goals on

1813   the retrieval process, such as a goal to suppress traumatic memory to protect mood

1814   (Mamat et al., 2024) or to maximise accuracy in a court of law. While retrieved-

1815   context models have successfully accounted for temporal dynamics of recall even

1816   outside the lab, their evidence base comes mostly from intentional recall of words, so

1817   more evidence is needed to show that they provide the best explanation for one-shot

1818   learning of meaning-rich, sensory material, such as can occur after traumatic

1819   experiences. We note also that in Experiment 1 there were, surprisingly, more lab-

1820   based involuntary memories of the neutral film than the traumatic film, suggesting

1821   that it may be feasible to study involuntary memory in healthy samples using

1822   exclusively neutral stimulus material. Future research could start from simulations

1823   using retrieved-context models to define cases that best test model assumptions.

1825   Constraints on Generality

1826          The participants in this research were all healthy adults, screened specifically

1827   to ensure no prior history of, or heightened vulnerability to, psychological trauma.

1828   Owing to the experiments being conducted in-person at the University of Cambridge,

1829   these participants were also likely to have been predominantly from western,

1830   educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic (WEIRD) backgrounds (Henrich,

1831   Heine, & Norenzayan, 2010). These factors limit the generalisability of the present

1832   findings to distinct sample populations, including children and adolescents, as well

1833   as individuals from non-WEIRD cultures, and individuals experiencing trauma-related

1834   clinical conditions such as PTSD.

1835          These limitations were deemed justifiable, to ensure that this research could

1836   be conducted in compliance with ethical guidelines, using rigorously controlled in-

1837   person experiments, and could produce findings which were directly comparable with

1838   previous laboratory-based research in this area.
