# Goal-Directed Remembering When Context Cues Competitors: A Retrieved-Context Account of Selective Interference

##

I think this is the better general-memory-theory point:

> **How can goal-directed remembering remain selective when the context that normally cues the target has also become bound to competitors?**

That is more interesting than “don’t overinterpret dissociations,” which is already well-trod by Newell & Dunn, Dunn & Kirsner, Plaut, Benjamin, etc. The dissociation-inference critique can motivate caution, but it should not be the main theoretical contribution.

The broader claim could be:

> Context binding is useful because it lets context cue event memories, but it also creates interference when later material is encoded into a reinstated target context. Deliberate recall is protected when retrieval can construct or use a more diagnostic basis for target access than the compromised context alone.

That makes trauma-film selective interference a worked case of a general retrieval problem:

1. A reminder reinstates context from an earlier event.
2. New task material becomes bound to that context.
3. Later context-guided retrieval now cues both target and competitor material.
4. Protection occurs when retrieval can avoid relying solely on that contaminated context.

The two “protection” cases then become unified rather than ad hoc:

- **Deliberate recall:** a maintained film-retrieval goal gives film material a selective source of support that task material does not share.
- **Recognition/test format:** the candidate item is already available, so retrieval does not first require the film item to win a context-guided candidate-selection competition.

So the general memory point is not:

> Selective interference is ambiguous, so DRA might be wrong.

It is closer to:

> Context-binding interference depends on the retrieval route. A context that has become less diagnostic can impair unguided recall, while goal-directed or item-presenting retrieval conditions can preserve access by supplying more selective target support.

That is a positive theory of episodic retrieval. It connects:

- contextual binding theory, where context binds event elements and supports later memory;
- retrieved-context models, where current context cues candidate items and recalled/presented items reinstate context;
- retrieval-control work, where deliberate recall can sustain target-oriented cues or monitoring;
- recognition theory, where a presented probe retrieves associated context rather than being selected from context.

For the draft, this would shift the center of gravity. The DRA paragraph becomes motivation, not the central fight. The central question becomes:

> When does context binding become interference, and what forms of retrieval protect against it?

The selective-interference literature matters because it gives a sharp empirical setting where all of this is visible: reminders, competitor encoding, intrusions, deliberate recall, recognition, VIT cues, and test-format heterogeneity.

Sources for the older dissociation-caution point are still useful, but only as background: Newell & Dunn argue dissociation logic is weak for dimensional inference; Dunn & Kirsner argue ordinary dissociations are weaker than reversed association; Plaut shows double dissociation without modularity; Benjamin gives the memory-domain version for recognition/process dissociations ([Newell & Dunn 2008](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1364661308001526), [Dunn & Kirsner 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3353477/), [Plaut 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7629273/), [Benjamin 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3045270/)). But your paper’s more distinctive contribution should be the retrieval-protection account, not the generic dissociation warning.

## 

Yes. That is the right formulation.

The question version is:

> How can goal-directed remembering remain selective when the context that normally cues the target has also become bound to competitors?

The theory-integration version is:

> We link a context-binding account of interference with accounts of goal- and cue-guided selective retrieval.

Those are not two different projects. They are two levels of the same project:

- **Context-binding account of interference** explains why the problem arises: a reminder reinstates target context, and task material encoded in that context later competes with the target.
- **Goal- and cue-guided selective retrieval** explains when the problem is overcome: deliberate goals, source/semantic cues, or presented items can discriminate target material from competitors.
- **Trauma-film selective interference** is the focal case: film context becomes less selective after reminder-linked task encoding, but voluntary memory can be protected when retrieval has another way to isolate film material.

A clean thesis sentence could be:

> We argue that selective interference in the trauma-film paradigm can be understood by linking context-binding interference with goal- and cue-guided selective retrieval: reminder-linked task encoding makes film-associated context cue competitors, whereas deliberate goals or presented items can protect retrieval when they discriminate film material from those competitors.

This gives the paper a stronger identity than “another interpretation,” because it says exactly which two theoretical pieces are being connected.

Yes. The general-memory-theory point is **not** “dissociations are underdetermined.” That is only a methodological caution.

The better point is:

> Goal-directed remembering is protected from interference only when retrieval can use a cue or control state that discriminates the target from competitors supported by the same context.

Or more fully:

> Context is normally useful because it helps cue the target episode. But when new material is encoded in a reinstated target context, that context becomes less selective: it now supports both target and competitor material. Deliberate recall can remain selective only if retrieval can recruit another basis for selection, such as source, semantic, sensory-feature, or item-presenting cues that favor the target without equally favoring the competitors.

That is the memory-theory contribution. It links two literatures that are usually separate:

1. **Retrieved-context / item-context binding:** explains how competitors become retrievable from the same context as the target.
2. **Goal- and cue-guided selective retrieval:** explains when deliberate retrieval can still isolate the target from those competitors.

The trauma-film selective-interference effect is then not mainly a dissociation-inference example. It is a case where this general problem becomes visible:

> reminder-linked task encoding can make film-related context cue task material, but voluntary memory can look spared when deliberate recall uses a film-directed cue or recognition presents the film item directly.

That framing also handles your two “conditions” cleanly:

- **Deliberate recall:** protection comes from constructing and maintaining a discriminating film-directed cue.
- **Recognition:** protection comes from the test presenting the candidate item, reducing the need to select film material from a context-supported competitor set.

So the paper’s broader thesis could be:

> Selective interference in the trauma-film paradigm reflects a general problem in episodic retrieval: context-bound competitors impair remembering when retrieval depends on a shared context, but their effect is reduced when goals or test cues provide a more diagnostic route to the target.

That is much stronger than “another interpretation of selective interference,” and it is not just a warmed-over dissociation critique.
