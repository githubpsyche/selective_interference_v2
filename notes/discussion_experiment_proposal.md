The cleanest experiment tests a boundary-condition claim:

> Selective interference should arise when later material becomes strongly associated with the target episode’s context but remains distinguishable from that episode by a goal-directed retrieval cue.

That is more precise than claiming that any intervening task should selectively reduce involuntary memory.

## The clean experiment

Use neutral, nameable episodic material so that success would demonstrate a general-memory phenomenon rather than another trauma-film effect.

1. Participants encode a focal episode or set, A.

2. Before encoding a second set of items, B, manipulate the active context:

   - A-context condition: reinstate A-associated context with an A reminder.
   - Shifted-context condition: use a matched reminder or activity that reinstates another context.

   Identical B materials are encoded in both conditions. B is never subsequently designated as a retrieval target.

3. Independently manipulate how A is tested:

   - Deliberate condition: participants are instructed to retrieve A.
   - Involuntary condition: participants perform a nonmemory cover task using the same external cues and report any experimental memories that arise spontaneously. A can therefore appear only as an intrusion.

4. Require externalized, source-classified reports. Every A or B candidate should be recorded with its output position and latency, including candidates rejected as off-target during deliberate recall.

The core design is therefore:

\[
\text{Context before B: A-associated vs shifted}
\times
\text{Retrieval of A: deliberate vs involuntary}
\]

The predicted selective interference effect is an interaction:

- Encoding B in A-associated context increases B’s accessibility and reduces involuntary A reports.
- The same manipulation has much less effect on deliberate A recall because an A-directed cue favors A over B.
- B itself should appear more frequently among reported candidates after A-context reinstatement, linking the loss of A intrusions to competition rather than trace damage.

The experiment should use separate, counterbalanced A episodes for the two retrieval modes so that deliberate testing cannot contaminate the involuntary test.

A stronger follow-up would manipulate how well the maintained goal cue discriminates A from B. If A and B share temporal context but have distinguishable source or category features, deliberate recall should be protected. If B also shares the target-defining features, deliberate protection should deteriorate. This would connect the contextual-binding account to Moccia and Morcom’s work showing that goal-directed selection depends on cues that discriminate targets from competitors.

The resulting program combines three previously separate strands:

- Hupbach-style reminder research supplies contextual competitor formation.
- Berntsen/Staugaard and Kobelt-style procedures supply matched deliberate and involuntary access.
- Moccia, Healey and Wahlheim, and externalized-recall research supply cue diagnosticity and observable control during search.

## Mechanism-level predictions

The core interaction establishes general selective interference. A richer version could distinguish the three control mechanisms:

- Start-context reinstatement should alter recall initiation and favor early A positions, but should not protect A from B when the reinstated context now supports both sets.
- Maintained A-directed support should reduce B entry throughout deliberate search, with protection varying systematically with cue diagnosticity.
- Retrieval monitoring should not necessarily prevent B from entering search. It should increase B rejection and reduce the tendency for one B candidate to lead to another.

Importantly, the sequence measures become genuinely diagnostic only when the relevant control variable is also manipulated. An early-output effect alone does not establish start-context reinstatement, and consistent A recall alone does not establish maintained A support.

## Ideas we discounted or reduced in importance

| Earlier idea | Why it initially appealed | Why we discounted it |
|---|---|---|
| Two-list externalized recall, with instructions to recall A or B | Makes the same A items targets in one condition and intrusions in another | When B is targeted, the competitor material receives direct goal support. Retrieval intentionality and competitor targeting are no longer independent. |
| Broad recall versus A-directed recall | Requires only A and B and resembles the model’s unguided condition | A items produced during broad recall are not genuinely unintended intrusions. Calling the resulting interaction “selective interference” would be vulnerable. |
| Add a third target list, C | Allows recall A versus recall C while B remains permanently off-target | Logically sound, but cumbersome. C exists only to create a retrieval goal in the intrusion condition. A nonmemory cover task can make A involuntary without introducing another list. |
| Interleave targetable categories within one list | Avoids the appearance of an artificial three-list design | Interleaving destroys the coherent temporal context associated with A. Using A’s category both to bind B and later to protect A also conflates contextual competition with the maintained goal cue. Blocked categories would work, but are functionally separate episodes again. |
| Use a second set of trauma-film clips as B | Keeps the experiment close to the trauma-film paradigm and makes B reportable | It is unclear that another film would reproduce the effect of imagery-competing tasks. It may also share too many source and semantic features with A for an A-directed cue to remain diagnostic. |
| Model a conventional list-before-last dataset | Provides target intrusions, serial positions, and sometimes externalized candidates | Existing designs generally make the intervening list a target in one condition and do not independently manipulate A-context reinstatement before B. They can test aspects of retrieval control, but not the full selective-interference interaction. |
| Treat Hupbach-style updating data as the generalization | Reminder-linked B intrusions are an excellent match to contextual competitor formation | A is only deliberately recalled. There is no matched condition in which A can appear solely as an involuntary intrusion. It supplies one half of the proposed experiment. |
| Treat Berntsen/Staugaard or Kobelt as the generalization | They offer controlled deliberate-versus-involuntary retrieval, often with identical cues | They lack independently manipulated, never-target B competitors encoded in A-associated versus shifted context. Cue repetition is cue overload, not the proposed temporal-context mechanism. |
| Use Moccia and Morcom as direct evidence | Their findings closely support the importance of cue diagnosticity | They test selective recollection, not contextual competitor formation or a selective interference effect. They motivate the maintained-cue prediction rather than test the whole account. |
| Use Pastötter et al. as the empirical analogue | Interpolated retrieval reduced prior-list intrusions without a corresponding later-memory loss | Retrieval status changes across test occasions, the intervention is not reportable B learning in reinstated A context, and suitable ordered archival data are unavailable. It is a suggestive precedent, not a decisive test. |
| Add emotion versus neutral simulations | Native to eCMR and connected to available trauma-film data | A simple emotional flag risks producing only generic memory-strength effects and confounds emotion with source discriminability. Useful as a robustness analysis, not the conceptual expansion. |
| Model repeated versus unique intrusions | Clinically important and well suited to retrieved-context dynamics across retrieval episodes | It requires a new recurrence model with repeated retrieval opportunities, trigger schedules, and item-level heterogeneity. It is a separate research program. |
| Expand recognition | Provides a clean test-format boundary condition | Recognition largely bypasses competitive selection by presenting the candidate. Rik reasonably regarded the simulation as comparatively trivial; it does little to establish the broader retrieval-control program. |

The Discussion should therefore present the factorial experiment above as a concrete untested prediction, while using the partial precedents to show that each component is independently grounded. The novelty is precisely that no existing paradigm appears to combine contextual competitor formation, independently manipulated retrieval intentionality, permanently off-target competitors, and observable retrieval sequences.

===

The cleanest proposal is a category-directed externalized-recall experiment that independently manipulates competitor formation, cue diagnosticity, and retrieval intention. It preserves the defining selective-interference contrast while moving it into ordinary episodic-memory search.

## Proposed experiment

1. **Initial study list.** Participants study two blocked semantic categories, such as bird names and occupation names. Their identities, roles, and presentation order are counterbalanced.

2. **Reminder manipulation.** After a delay, participants receive either a cue reminding them of the focal bird category or a matched control cue.

3. **Additional study list.** Participants encode nameable potential competitors:

   - furniture names, which are semantically distinguishable from birds;
   - mammal names, which share the broader animal category with birds.

4. **Externalized recall.** After a retention interval, the postcue instructs participants to recall either bird names or occupation names. They report every candidate that comes to mind, including off-target candidates, and classify its source.

Across recall conditions, the same bird items therefore become:

- intended recalls when birds are targeted;
- intrusions when occupations are targeted.

The additional-list items are never targets. This is crucial: the intervention material remains a potential competitor rather than becoming the goal of one retrieval condition.

## Critical prediction

The reminder should link the additional-list items to bird-associated context. The subsequent bird-recall goal can nevertheless protect deliberate recall when it discriminates bird names from those competitors.

Thus:

- With **furniture competitors**, reminder-linked learning should reduce bird intrusions more than deliberate bird recall. This is the general-memory analogue of selective interference.
- With **mammal competitors**, the bird-directed cue should be less diagnostic because targets and competitors share animal features. Deliberate recall should consequently lose some of its protection.

The decisive result is not merely better recall when birds are targeted. It is an interaction: the competitor manipulation affects focal items less when they are deliberately targeted than when they arise as intrusions. Competitor category should moderate that interaction.

A reminder-only or no-additional-list condition would be a useful diagnostic control for any direct mnemonic effect of reminding, although it need not belong to the core factorial design.

## What this could reveal about the three mechanisms

Externalized response order, latency, input position, and source classification would permit stronger tests than aggregate recall alone:

- **Start-context reinstatement** should alter recall initiation and favor early positions in the focal block, but should not protect against competitors that have become associated with that same context.
- **Maintained category support** should protect focal recall specifically when the focal cue discriminates targets from competitors. Its signature should therefore depend on the furniture–mammal manipulation.
- **Retrieval monitoring** should primarily affect what happens after an off-target candidate enters search, including whether search continues through the competitor list or returns to the target category.

This is not equivalent to spontaneous memories arising during daily life. Attention is directed inward during externalized recall. That limitation is also an advantage here: deliberate recalls and intrusions are measured within the same memory-search procedure rather than through diary reports versus recognition or recall tests.

## How the literature supports the proposal

The experiment combines lines of work that have mostly remained separate:

- Reminder and retrieved-context research explains how later learning can become associated with a reinstated earlier context.
- Moccia and Morcom’s work motivates the prediction that goal-directed selection depends on whether available cues discriminate targets from competitors.
- Externalized and specialized-recall work from Healey, Wahlheim, Lohnas, and colleagues supplies methods for observing off-target retrievals and distinguishing initiation, sustained selection, and monitoring.

The contribution would be to cross these principles in one design: newly learned competitors acquire access through focal context, while their semantic relation to the retrieval goal determines whether deliberate recall can overcome that competition.

## Attractive ideas we discounted

| Earlier idea | Why it was attractive | Why it was insufficient |
|---|---|---|
| Broad recall versus focal-list recall | Very simple comparison of unguided and directed search | Focal items in broad recall are not genuinely unintended or off-target, making “selective interference” unconvincing. |
| Recall List A versus Recall competitor List B | Makes A items targets in one condition and intrusions in another | B becomes the retrieval target, conflating the material producing interference with the alternative retrieval goal. |
| Three full sets: alternative C, focal A, competitor B | Logically clean because A changes status while B is never targeted | Cumbersome and inefficient. The two blocked categories in one initial list provide the same logical roles more economically. |
| Randomly interleaved focal and alternative categories | Economical and naturally supports category instructions | The focal category lacks a coherent temporal context. Interference becomes category/source based rather than the temporal context-to-item binding proposed by the model. |
| A second trauma-film set as the competitor | Keeps the experiment close to the trauma-film paradigm and creates reportable competitors | Another film is not known to reproduce the imagery-competing intervention effect and could increase rather than selectively reduce focal-film intrusions. |
| Deep versus shallow encoding of identical competitor words | Directly manipulates how well competitors are learned | Semantic versus perceptual judgments also alter semantic and source representations, confounding competitor strength with the very cue diagnosticity being tested. |
| Context manipulation followed by an alternative target list | Appeared capable of changing focal intrusions through contextual overlap | If the alternative target is encoded in focal context, retrieved-context theory can predict more focal intrusions when that alternative is recalled, the opposite of the required effect. |
| Existing externalized dual-list datasets | Some contain output order, source errors, and positional information useful for mechanism identification | None located so far also contains an independent competitor-learning manipulation and makes the same focal material a target or intrusion under a matched test. |
| Pastötter et al.’s intervention study | Closest published positive analogue: fewer prior-list intrusions without poorer later deliberate recall | Intentional status changes across immediate and cumulative tests rather than through matched instructions, and suitable response-level data do not appear publicly available. |

The current proposal is therefore not merely another list-before-last design. It combines the useful part of that logic—changing whether the same material is target or intrusion—with a separate, never-targeted competitor list and an explicit manipulation of whether the retrieval goal can distinguish those competitors.
