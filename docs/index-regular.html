Abstract

Memory theory must explain why interference can affect deliberate and involuntary remembering differently. Studies using distressing films show that a film reminder followed by an imagery-competing task such as Tetris can reduce later spontaneous memories of the film (intrusions) while leaving voluntary memory relatively intact. This selective interference effect is often interpreted through clinical theories of traumatic memory in which intrusive and voluntary remembering depend on different memory representations. We develop an alternative account in an emotional Context Maintenance and Retrieval (eCMR) model that predicts sequences of film and non-film recall events. Events become associated with context, a changing internal representation of experience. A film reminder reinstates film-associated context, so mental imagery generated during the subsequent task becomes associated with that context. During later retrieval, task memories compete with film memories cued by the same context, reducing film recall without weakening the original film associations. During deliberate recall, however, we propose that people pursue the goal of remembering the film by constructing and maintaining a retrieval cue that distinguishes film from task material. In simulations, sustained support from this cue was required to reproduce the selective-interference pattern across reminder and task conditions, protecting deliberate recall relative to involuntary retrieval. These results suggest that competitor learning and retrieval cueing can produce selective interference within a shared memory system, motivating tests in other memory tasks.

Introduction

Traumatic experience foregrounds a tension between accessibility and selectivity in episodic memory. From a functional perspective, events with lasting significance need to remain accessible in memory so remembered details can guide later decisions (Zhou et al., 2025). At the same time, adaptive retrieval is selective: what comes to mind should normally depend on current goals, cues, and circumstances. Intrusive memories after trauma show how highly accessible event memories can strain the selectivity of retrieval. These intrusions arise involuntarily and often take the form of brief visual images of moments from the traumatic event. In posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), such memories can come to mind unbidden in inappropriate circumstances, causing distress and interfering with ongoing activity (Brewin et al., 2010; Ehlers & Clark, 2000; Iyadurai et al., 2019; Krans et al., 2009). Explaining how episodic memory keeps consequential event memories accessible while regulating their retrieval is therefore a shared target for both clinical science and mechanistic theories of memory.

The trauma-film paradigm provides a controlled setting for comparing intrusive and voluntary memory for the same material (Holmes & Bourne, 2008; James et al., 2016; see McConnell, Leehr, et al., 2026, for recent methodological guidance). Participants view a distressing film, record later intrusions as a measure of involuntary memory, and often complete voluntary-memory tests of its contents. This design makes it possible to ask whether the same material can remain accessible to voluntary retrieval while becoming more or less likely to come to mind involuntarily. In such research, intrusion frequency is often only weakly related to voluntary-memory performance (Holmes et al., 2004; James et al., 2016; Lau-Zhu et al., 2019, 2021). Memory for the same film can therefore arise involuntarily or be retrieved voluntarily, and these two outcomes can be largely independent.

Researchers have sharpened this dissociation using the imagery-competing task intervention (ICTI) and related procedures. In an ICTI, participants receive brief reminders of the target event and then complete a task designed to compete with the visual imagery those reminders call to mind (Highfield et al., 2025). Imagery-competing tasks administered within hours of film viewing have been shown to reduce later film intrusions, but leave voluntary memory for the same material relatively intact (Asselbergs et al., 2024; Deeprose et al., 2012; Holmes et al., 2009, 2010; Lau-Zhu et al., 2019, 2021). In delayed variants (i.e. when administered a day or more after film viewing), imagery-competing task procedures can reduce intrusions recorded in a diary over several days, without affecting performance of a deliberate test of recognition memory for scenes from the films (James et al., 2015; Kessler et al., 2020). Clinical studies have also found that ICTIs reduce intrusive memories after real-world trauma (Beckenstrom et al., 2026; Iyadurai et al., 2023; Kanstrup et al., 2024; Kessler et al., 2018; Ramineni et al., 2023). The pattern of reduced intrusions alongside relatively preserved voluntary memory is termed ‘the selective interference effect’ (Lau-Zhu et al., 2019, 2021). Explaining this effect within a broader theory of episodic memory search is the central aim of the present work.

One prominent existing explanation holds that intrusive and voluntary remembering depend on separate memory representations that are differentially vulnerable to interference (Brewin et al., 1996; Brewin, 2014). Dual-representation accounts give this interpretation an architectural basis. They postulate that voluntary recollection arises from hippocampally supported representations that bind event elements within a spatiotemporal context (Bird et al., 2012; Bisby et al., 2020), whereas intrusive re-experiencing arises from activation of sensory and affective representations supported by amygdala and sensory systems (Bisby et al., 2020; Brewin et al., 2010). When a visuospatial task follows a film reminder soon after film viewing, it is proposed to interfere with consolidation of the sensory-perceptual representation (Holmes et al., 2009, 2010). When the procedure is delayed after film viewing (e.g. 24 hours later), a reminder cue is given in order to reactivate the relevant memory before the task, with the interference procedure proposed to alter later availability through reconsolidation or post-reactivation updating (James et al., 2015; Kindt et al., 2009). The selective interference effect is therefore interpreted as evidence that the intervention disrupts the sensory-bound representations that support intrusions more strongly than the contextualized representations that support voluntary recollection (Deeprose et al., 2012; Lau-Zhu et al., 2019).

Here we argue that preserved voluntary memory does not, by itself, establish that a separate representation was spared. More generally, differences in retrieval outcomes need not reveal differences in what was stored. The selective interference effect can instead arise from differences in how the same memory is cued and controlled at retrieval.

Retrieved-context models offer a general theory of episodic memory search in which context cues associated events, including memories that a person did not intend to retrieve (Cohen & Kahana, 2022; Lohnas et al., 2015; Polyn et al., 2011). Within this framework, goal-directed remembering involves controlling retrieval—for example, by selecting cues that favor the information one intends to remember. Although recent extensions formalize particular forms of targeted search (Healey & Wahlheim, 2024; Lohnas, 2025), a general account of how retrieval goals and test format regulate competition during memory search remains unresolved (Healey & Uitvlugt, 2019; Lohnas et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2025). We address this gap by comparing deliberate recall and deliberate recognition with involuntary retrieval; all generated from the same learned associations. We characterize how control mechanisms can make film recall less susceptible to interference when retrieval is deliberate rather than involuntary.

In our account, events become associated with current context, a gradually changing mental representation of recent experience (Howard & Kahana, 2002; Polyn et al., 2009a). Film-associated context may remain active immediately after film viewing, or become active again (reinstated) following a later reminder. Imagery generated during the visuospatial task while this context is active becomes linked to it. When this context later guides recall, it therefore cues memories of both the task and the film, placing them in competition. The recall and recognition simulations both focus on the delayed case, comparing conditions with and without a film reminder before task encoding.

How strongly this competition affects performance depends on test format and retrieval control. Deliberate recognition can be less affected because such tests present candidate items for judgment rather than requiring each item to be brought to mind through context-guided search. Deliberate recall still depends on context-guided search, so memories of the task can compete with memories of the film. We propose that people pursue the goal of recalling the film by constructing and maintaining a mental retrieval cue that distinguishes film material from task material. This cue can give film candidates an additional source of support without equally supporting task competitors.

We formalize this retrieval account in an emotional variant (Cohen & Kahana, 2022; Talmi et al., 2019) of the Context Maintenance and Retrieval (CMR) model (Polyn et al., 2009a). To represent the distressing material used in trauma-film studies, this variant includes a component of context that distinguishes emotional from neutral experiences. Memories of individual emotional events can form stronger associations with this shared component of context, increasing the support it provides during retrieval.

We use eCMR to evaluate a retrieved-context account of selective interference in three simulations that explore the roles of test format and retrieval control in memory output. Intentionality and Test Format in Selective Interference

Empirical tests of selective interference combine various intrusive memory measures with various types of voluntary memory test. Diary measures record intrusions that arise during daily life, whereas laboratory intrusion tasks collect reports during a defined monitoring period, sometimes while film cues are present. Voluntary memory is commonly assessed either by recall tests, which require participants to bring film details to mind, or by recognition tests, which present verbal or visual candidates for judgment (James et al., 2016; Lau-Zhu et al., 2019, 2021). These combinations vary in both retrieval intentionality and test format. Figure 2 illustrates selected examples from the literature. The final row shows Experiment 1 of McConnell et al. (2026), which tested voluntary and involuntary memory under closely matched laboratory conditions. They did not find the expected reduction in involuntary memories after the visuospatial task relative to the control condition. Both the selective interference effect and failures to observe it constrain a theoretical account.

Many prominent demonstrations of selective interference compare diary or laboratory intrusion reports with recognition tests (Holmes et al., 2009, 2010; James et al., 2015; Lau-Zhu et al., 2019, 2021). Such comparisons confound retrieval intentionality with test format. Intrusion reports depend on film material coming to mind, whereas recognition measures the ability to judge material supplied by the test. Preserved recognition therefore does not establish that film material remains similarly accessible during deliberate recall, or that intentionality alone produced the dissociation.

Free recall permits a more direct comparison with intrusion reports because both outcomes require film material to be brought to mind before it can be reported. Selective interference has been observed with free recall, showing that the effect is not confined to recognition-based comparisons (Lau-Zhu et al., 2019). Comparing free recall with diary or laboratory intrusion reports therefore more directly tests whether goal-directed retrieval preserves access to film material. Accordingly, we use involuntary versus deliberate recall to examine retrieval control and deliberate recognition to examine the separate contribution of test format.

Earlier CMR models did not explicitly compare deliberate and involuntary recall of the same material. Originally constructed to explain deliberate recall, the models used the same mechanisms to account for recall from the target list and mistaken recalls from outside the target list (Lohnas et al., 2015; Polyn et al., 2009a). CMR3 classifies recalls from the intended context as voluntary and recalls from other contexts as “involuntary intrusions”, and uses contextual information to filter out unwanted memories before they are reported (Cohen & Kahana, 2022). This classification does not, by itself, explain how a retrieval goal changes the course of search. To our knowledge, this is the first retrieved-context model to compare susceptibility to interference during involuntary and deliberate recall of the same material. We test which control operations reduce interference from task memories during deliberate recall while holding learned associations constant.

A Retrieved-Context Account of Selective Interference

Retrieved-context models explain recall as a search guided by a changing context state that is shaped by recent experience and prior retrieval. In CMR, bidirectional associations link items and context: context cues candidate items, while retrieved items and items presented at test reinstate the contexts associated with them (Howard & Kahana, 2002; Polyn et al., 2009a, 2009b; Sederberg et al., 2008). We first describe how these operations organize deliberate and involuntary recall. We then use this framework to explain how task material becomes a competitor for film recall and how deliberate recall controls that competition.

How Context Organizes Recall

Retrieved-context models were developed most prominently from laboratory free-recall studies in which participants deliberately study and recall lists of words. In this setting, recall favors recently studied items, and successive recalls tend to come from nearby study positions (Howard & Kahana, 2002; Kahana, 1996; Sederberg et al., 2008). Recency follows because retrieval context remains similar to contexts from recent study and recall events. Temporal contiguity follows because recalling an item reinstates context from its study position, which resembles the contexts associated with nearby items. Temporal contiguity is not confined to this setting. It has been observed in autobiographical recall, when words were delivered hourly by smartphone, and following an audio-guided museum tour (Cortis Mack et al., 2017; Diamond & Levine, 2020; Moreton & Ward, 2010). Temporal organization is weaker but still detectable after incidental encoding, a pattern that retrieved-context models can capture (Healey, 2018; Mundorf et al., 2021). The present account therefore extends the same contextual cuing and reinstatement mechanisms to memory for an incidentally encoded film.

Recall is also organized by shared content, task, and source. Semantically related items, items encoded under the same task, and items from the same source can cluster during recall (Lohnas et al., 2023; Morton & Polyn, 2016; Polyn et al., 2009a, 2009b, 2012). CMR captures semantic and source organization by allowing each form of information to contribute to the context state that guides retrieval. A target-category or source cue can therefore favor one subset of items even when those items were encountered at different times. Temporal associations can still organize retrieval within that subset (Lohnas et al., 2026; Polyn et al., 2011).

Emotional memory gives source organization a further role in the model used here. Emotional items can cluster together, and emotional material is often remembered better than neutral material (Talmi et al., 2007; Talmi, 2013; Talmi & Moscovitch, 2004). Emotional CMR represents affective information as a source-like context dimension and allows emotional items to form stronger source-context associations (Cohen & Kahana, 2022; Talmi et al., 2019). In the present account, film events therefore share affective source information while remaining distinct items with different temporal associations.

How Task Material Competes for Recall

The same contextual processes that organize target recall can also bring material outside the intended target into memory. In multi-list free recall, participants sometimes report items from earlier lists instead of the list they were asked to recall (Howard et al., 2008; Lohnas et al., 2015; Zaromb et al., 2006). Those prior-list intrusions are structured: they are biased toward recently studied prior lists and vary with intervening study or delay. Retrieved-context models explain this structure because current context can continue to support items from recent or related episodes. Although that support declines with further experience, some non-target items remain candidates for recall.

In the model, film, task, and delay periods are represented as sequences of potentially recallable events that update context. Film events correspond roughly to brief moments in the film, such as an approaching car or the moment of an accident. Particularly distressing moments (“hotspots”) can later recur as mental images (Singh et al., 2022). Task events represent visuospatial imagery generated during the task, such as imagining how a Tetris block will rotate or fit into a gap. Delay events represent other intervening experiences in the hours or days after film viewing, such as noticing a passer-by while walking home. The model distinguishes individual events and their sources but does not explicitly represent the visual content or similarity of film and task imagery.

Task events become competitors when they are encoded while film-associated context is active, either soon after the film or after a reminder reinstates that context. When participants return to an earlier learning context, new material can become linked to the earlier episode (Hupbach et al., 2008; Sederberg et al., 2011). In an fMRI study, greater reinstatement of an earlier learning context during new learning predicted later misattribution of new items to the earlier episode (Gershman et al., 2013). Task events can therefore acquire associations with the context that also supports film events. The present simulations focus on the delayed case, using a film reminder to reinstate film-associated context before task encoding and varying how strongly task events become associated with that context. This manipulation adds task competitors without changing the film-item associations formed during film encoding. Panel B of Figure 3 summarizes this encoding structure.

Retrieval Control in Deliberate Recall

Once target and competitor memories are both available, explaining deliberate recall requires specifying how a retrieval goal shapes the course of search. The present comparison holds learned associations constant across involuntary and deliberate recall. Any difference in their susceptibility to competition must therefore arise from control applied during deliberate retrieval. We consider three candidate processes that alter successive stages of search: orienting search toward the target episode before sampling, maintaining target-selective support during sampling, and limiting the influence of an off-target memory after it is sampled. The following paragraphs develop these processes in the order in which they act.

Retrieval Orientation

A retrieval goal (an intention to remember particular information) may first orient memory search toward target material. When people are asked to recall an earlier studied list after studying a more recent one, their early responses are often already biased toward the instructed list rather than showing a gradual search backward through recent-list items (Healey & Wahlheim, 2024; Lohnas et al., 2015). Recall also shows a reliable tendency to start near the beginning of a studied list, especially when the usual end-of-list advantage is weakened by a delay (Healey & Kahana, 2014; Herrema & Kahana, 2024; Howard & Kahana, 1999). Several models account for these patterns by using list-related or pre-list context to cue recall (Cornell & Zhang, 2025; Healey & Wahlheim, 2024; Logan, 2021; Morton & Polyn, 2016).

Like study items, instructions identifying a list can acquire contextual associations during encoding. When presented before the list, an instruction also contributes to the context in which early items are encoded. A later instruction identifying the same list can reactivate its representation and associated context before recall begins (Healey & Wahlheim, 2024). In the present simulations, we represent the effect of the film-recall instruction by shifting context toward the state present when film encoding began. This candidate tests whether orienting the start of search toward the target episode can reduce the effect of task competition once ordinary context-guided sampling resumes.

Retrieval Cue Maintenance

Orienting the start of search does not determine which candidates receive support as search unfolds. A retrieval goal may therefore also remain active during sampling, selectively supporting target memories. When people study mixed-category material and are asked to retrieve only one category, they can restrict recall to the target subset while temporal organization remains visible within that subset (Lohnas et al., 2026; Polyn et al., 2011). In this kind of task, intracranial recordings have revealed that category-selective activity tracks retrieval effort, remains active between reported recalls, and weakens before extra-category intrusions (Norman et al., 2017). Retrieved-context models provide a formal precedent for this kind of non-temporal support: semantic, task, and source information can contribute to candidate support alongside temporal context (Morton & Polyn, 2016; Polyn et al., 2009a, 2009b). Category-directed search has been formalized in associative memory models, and recent review work treats this as a natural but still underdeveloped direction for CMR (Lohnas et al., 2026; Raaijmakers & Shiffrin, 1980). A maintained film-category cue implements this form of control by adding support to film candidates without equally supporting task competitors. Because task candidates remain available through their associations with the current context, this candidate tests whether continuing target-selective support can reduce the effect of competition without removing the competitors.

Retrieval Monitoring

Even target-selective support need not prevent an off-target memory from being sampled. Retrieval monitoring acts after a candidate has been sampled. Deliberate recall may therefore also involve monitoring sampled candidates and limiting their influence on subsequent search. Evidence for this possibility comes from externalized recall tasks where participants report every word that comes to mind and mark errors. These instructions reveal many more intrusions than standard recall, and many sampled intrusions are marked as errors, indicating that off-target candidates can enter retrieval without being accepted as target memories (Lohnas et al., 2015; Unsworth et al., 2010). Participants were generally less likely to identify an intrusion as an error when they had misclassified their preceding response (Unsworth et al., 2010). Sampled intrusions can also pull search away from the target list, as intrusions tend to cluster with other intrusions from related prior-list or semantic contexts (Unsworth et al., 2010; Zaromb et al., 2006). Retrieved-context implementations have treated monitoring as a check on whether a retrieved candidate belongs to the target set, while still allowing rejected items to update the next retrieval context (Lohnas et al., 2015). Retrieval monitoring is implemented here by limiting how strongly a sampled non-film candidate updates context without changing the support that allowed it to be sampled. This candidate tests whether limiting how much an off-target retrieval redirects subsequent search can help deliberate recall recover after competition has already occurred. Deliberate Recognition

The control mechanisms considered above alter context-guided recall. Recognition instead uses the model’s bidirectional item-context associations in the opposite direction. During recall, current context cues candidate items, so a film item must be selected from candidates that can include task competitors. During recognition, a presented probe reinstates context associated with earlier encounters, and recognition evidence depends on the match between that context and the context active at test (Healey & Kahana, 2016; Jin et al., 2024). Task items therefore do not compete to be selected in place of the film probe, although task learning may still affect the contextual evidence it retrieves.

Taken together, the account separates what is learned from how it is later retrieved. Task events can become linked to film-associated context, while retrieval goals and test operations determine how strongly those associations affect access to film material. Selective interference can therefore emerge within a common episodic memory system without requiring intrusive and voluntary remembering to depend on separately vulnerable representations.

Model Specification

The formal model places film and non-film material within a common item-context memory. During recall, both enter the same sampling process, so task competition can alter what is retrieved while the associations supporting film memory remain available (Polyn et al., 2009a; Sederberg et al., 2008). We first specify the representations and learning rules that constitute this memory. The remaining subsections define the manipulations of reminder presence and task association strength used in Simulation 1. Task association strength determines how strongly the context present during task encoding later cues memories of the task. We then specify the operation used to model deliberate recognition in Simulation 2 and the retrieval control mechanisms compared in Simulation 3.

The formal model represents this sampling process with separate item and context representations. Each encoded event at position \(i\) is represented by an item feature vector \(f_{i}\), a one-hot vector that distinguishes that event from other encoded events in the simulated paradigm. Context \(c_{i}\) denotes the internal context state after event \(i\) has updated context. As film, task, and filler events occur, they update context and become associated with the context active around them. During recall, the current context state determines which candidate items receive support.

During encoding, items and states of context are linked through bidirectional associations represented in two matrices. Context-to-feature memory, \(M^{CF}\), maps context states to item support, allowing current context to cue candidate items during recall. Feature-to-context memory, \(M^{FC}\), maps item features to context states so that cued, reminded, or recalled items can reinstate associated context. All vectors are treated as column vectors, so \(M^{FC}f_{i}\) is a context vector and \(M^{CF}c_{i}\) is a feature-support vector.

Context and corresponding associations are further subdivided into temporal and source components. Temporal context distinguishes unique items so different film, task, and filler events can produce distinct context states even if items come from the same phase of the paradigm or share other features. Source context represents coarser information shared across related events, allowing recall to be shaped by task, semantic, or source relationships among items (Polyn et al., 2009a, 2009b). Emotional CMR variants distinguish emotional from neutral material in source context and give emotionally salient items stronger source-context associations (Cohen & Kahana, 2022; Talmi et al., 2019). In the present implementation, each item also has a source feature vector \(s_{i}\). Film items share a film-source feature, whereas task and filler items carry a neutral/default source feature.

Encoding the Paradigm Sequence

Film, task, and filler events become later recall candidates through context updating and item-context learning. Each event updates context and becomes associated with the resulting state. During later recall, candidate support depends on how strongly studied items are associated with the context states active during retrieval.

Before the simulated film starts, context is initialized to \(c_{0}\), a start state with no item-associated context units active. Memory matrices are initialized with pre-experimental associations that reflect baseline structure already present before the simulated paradigm begins. In \(M^{FC}\), each item \(i\) can cue its paired context unit with weight \(1 - \gamma\). In \(M^{CF}\), each item is most strongly supported by its paired context unit, with weight \(\delta\). The other item-associated context units provide weaker background support to that item, with weight \(\alpha\). The start state \(c_{0}\) initially provides no item support in \(M^{CF}\). The initial entries of \(M^{FC}\) and \(M^{CF}\) are therefore:

\[M_{pre}^{FC}(j,i) = \left\{ \begin{matrix} 1 - \gamma & \text{if }j = i + 1 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{matrix} \right.\ \quad\quad(1)\]

\[M_{pre}^{CF}(i,j) = \left\{ \begin{matrix} 0 & \text{if }j = 0 \\ \delta & \text{if }j = i + 1 \\ \alpha & \text{if }j > 0\text{ and }j \neq i + 1 \end{matrix} \right.\ \quad\quad(2)\]

When event \(i\) is encountered, its representation \(f_{i}\) retrieves context input through feature-to-context memory:

\[c_{i}^{IN} = M^{FC}f_{i}\quad\quad(3)\]

Context then drifts toward the normalized input. The coefficient \(\rho_{i}\) keeps context normalized after the update:

\[c_{i} = \rho_{i}c_{i - 1} + \beta_{enc}{\widehat{c}}_{i}^{IN}\quad\quad(4)\]

Each event also encodes associations between its item features and the current context state. Feature-to-context learning updates \(M^{FC}\) so later cues or sampled items can reinstate associated context:

\[\Delta M^{FC} = \gamma c_{i}f_{i}^{\top}\quad\quad(5)\]

Context-to-feature learning updates \(M^{CF}\) so later context can support the item during sampling:

\[\Delta M^{CF} = \phi_{i}f_{i}c_{i}^{\top}\quad\quad(6)\]

Here, \(\gamma\) controls feature-to-context learning. To capture primacy effects in recall, \(\phi_{i}\) starts at \(1 + \phi_{s}\) for the first encoded event in the paradigm. Its increment above 1 decreases by a factor of \(e^{- \phi_{d}}\) with each subsequent event.

Encoding events also have a source feature vector \(s_{i}\) that distinguishes film items from non-film items. The source component of context follows the same normalized update rule as temporal context, with source drift rate \(\beta_{emot}\). All items can form associations with the source context active for that event, scaled by the baseline learning parameter \(\eta_{s}\). For film encoding events, learning from source context to item features receives an additional scaling term, \(\phi_{emot}\), to reflect stronger encoding of emotional material in the trauma-film paradigm (Cohen & Kahana, 2022; Talmi et al., 2019). Task and filler events carry the neutral/default source feature, so they use \(\eta_s\) without the additional \(\phi_{emot}\) term. After encoding, film, task, and filler candidates can all enter later recall through the temporal and source-context associations learned for each item.

Task Encoding After a Film Reminder

After the delay following the film, the simulated paradigm can present film reminders before the task begins. The reminder phase reinstates film-associated context before task items are encoded. Task items encoded from that state can later receive support for recall from context that also supports film material.

The reminder phase begins by updating context toward the start state of the film sequence. Let \(c_{b}\) denote the context state after the break period. This initial update is:

\[c_{r,0} = \rho_{r,0}c_{b} + \beta_{rem,start}c_{0}.\quad\quad(7)\]

The model then applies a sequence of film-item reminder cues. Each item from the film phase is presented as a reminder cue in the order it was encoded. Feature-to-context memory retrieves reminder input as in Equation 3, and context drifts toward that input using the normalized update in Equation 4 with the reminder drift rate. Reminder cues update context but do not become recallable items and do not add new context-to-feature learning. This isolates the reminder’s effect to reinstating film-associated context rather than adding new film-item associations.

Task events are then encoded using the ordinary learning rules, starting from the context reached after the reminders. Task items carry the neutral/default source feature rather than the film-source feature, so they do not receive the added \(\phi_{emot}\) term. To vary task association strength, the simulations apply a multiplier \(m_{task}\ \)to context-to-feature learning for task events:

\[\Delta M_{task}^{CF} = m_{task}\phi_{i}f_{i}c_{i}^{\top}\quad\quad(8)\]

This sensitivity manipulation determines how strongly the context active during task encoding later supports task items during recall. Task items encoded after the reminder become competitors in later recall because they are associated with context states that are also linked to film material. The associations formed during film encoding remain unchanged.

Context-Guided Recall and Test-Phase Cues

Involuntary and deliberate recall both require film items to be sampled from a candidate set that also includes task and filler items. At sampling step j, the current context state assigns support to each candidate by probing context-to-feature memory:

\[a_{i,j} = \left\lbrack M^{CF}c_{j} \right\rbrack_{i}\quad\quad(9)\]

The context vector \(c_{j}\) includes both temporal and source-context components. By default, candidates already sampled in the current recall period are excluded from later sampling.

Sampling follows a Luce choice rule over the remaining candidates (Luce, 1959):

\[P(i \mid continue) = \frac{a_{i,j}^{\tau}}{\sum_{remaining\ k}^{}a_{k,j}^{\tau}}\quad\quad(10)\]

Following likelihood-based CMR implementations, stopping is modeled as a position-dependent hazard independent of current candidate support (Kragel et al., 2015; Morton & Polyn, 2016):

\[P_{stop}(j) = \theta_{s}e^{j\theta_{r}}\quad\quad(11)\]

This value is bounded to remain a valid probability. Because stopping is independent of candidate support, interference is read from the identity of sampled items. A sampled task or filler item takes the place of a possible film sample.

After a candidate is sampled, feature-to-context memory updates temporal and source context separately toward the sampled item’s associated context. For the temporal component, identified by the superscript \(temp\), the update is:

\[c_{j + 1}^{temp} = \rho_{rec,j}^{temp}c_{j}^{temp} + \beta_{rec}\widehat{\left( M^{FC}f_{r_{j}} \right)^{temp}}\quad\quad(12)\]

The source component follows the same update with its own drift rate, \(\beta_{rec,src}\), fixed at 1. The retrieved input and updated context are normalized separately for each component. Together, these updates let sampled material shape later search. A sampled film item can keep later sampling near film-associated context. A sampled task or filler item can pull later sampling toward non-film material.

Test-phase film cues use the same feature-to-context operation without becoming recall outputs. When a film cue \(q\) is presented, the cued item updates context through \(M^{FC}f_{q}\). The cue is not counted as a recalled item, does not make the cued film item unavailable for later recall, and adds no new learning. Recall sampling then resumes from the updated context state.

Deliberate Recall Control Mechanisms

Deliberate recall uses the same sampling rule as involuntary recall, with control settings applied before, during, or after sampling. The control settings only change post-encoding processes, including the retrieval cue, candidate support, or the influence of a sampled item on the context used for subsequent retrieval. Crucially, they do not change the film and task associations learned during encoding. This lets the simulations distinguish retrieval operations that increase film recall overall from those that reduce competition from task items encoded after a film reminder.

Start-of-film context reinstatement drifts context toward the state present at the beginning of film encoding before the first sampling step. The retrieval context is drifted toward the context state used to begin the film sequence, with drift rate \(\beta_{start}\). This can bias early sampling toward film candidates rather than starting from the ongoing context state produced by the final delay phase of the paradigm. This control mechanism can therefore increase film recall. However, the reinstated context can also support task items encoded after the reminder.

During deliberate recall, the model can maintain a film-category cue that is absent during involuntary recall. This cue abstracts the kind of semantic or category support used in retrieved-context models of selective recall, rather than updating temporal or source context during retrieval (Morton & Polyn, 2016; Polyn et al., 2011). The cue adds support to candidates marked as film items during sampling:

\[a_{i,j}^{cat} = a_{i,j} + \omega_{cat}g_{i}\quad\quad(13)\]

Here, \(g_{i} = 1\) for film candidates and \(g_{i} = 0\) for task and filler candidates. The indicator \(g_{i}\) is a retrieval-control cue, distinct from the source feature vector \(s_{i}\) used during encoding. Task and filler candidates can still compete through \(a_{i,j}\), but only film candidates receive the additional support from the maintained cue. Therefore, a maintained film-category cue can reduce the effect of those task competitors on film recall.

Externalized free recall asks participants to report every word that comes to mind during recall and identify intrusions or repetitions, revealing nontarget memories that enter search even when they are recognized as errors. In a retrieved-context model of this procedure (Lohnas et al., 2015), monitoring occurs after sampling. A candidate is accepted as a target response when its retrieved context sufficiently matches the context associated with the target list. Whether accepted or not, the candidate updates the context guiding the next sample. The present simulations retain this timing but change the consequence of monitoring. Sampled task or filler candidates update the next retrieval context less strongly. Let \(\kappa_{i} = 1\) for film candidates and \(\kappa_{i} = \kappa\) for task and filler candidates. Monitoring scales both retrieval drift rates by \(\kappa_{i}\). The temporal-context update becomes:

\[c_{j + 1}^{temp} = \rho_{j}^{temp}\left( \kappa_{i}\beta_{rec} \right)c_{j}^{temp} + \kappa_{i}\beta_{rec}\widehat{\left( M^{FC}f_{r_{j}} \right)^{temp}}\quad\quad(14)\]

The corresponding source-context update uses \(\kappa_{i}\beta_{rec,src}\). When \(\kappa < 1\), sampled non-film candidates still count as sampled outputs but both context components are updated less strongly toward their associated context. This reduces carryover from sampled task or filler candidates without changing the candidate support that made them available. Like start-of-film reinstatement, this operation does not by itself remove task support from film-associated context.

The three evaluated control mechanisms therefore enter the same retrieval cycle at different points: start-of-film context reinstatement before sampling, a maintained film-category cue during sampling, and retrieval monitoring after sampling.

Item Recognition

CMR has been used mainly to model recall, with relatively few extensions to recognition (Healey & Kahana, 2016; Jin et al., 2024; Lohnas et al., 2015). Here, recognition is represented only at the level of memory evidence: the model does not specify a decision criterion or generate an old/new response. A presented probe q retrieves associated context through feature-to-context memory, the same pathway used by reminders, cues, and sampled items. Recognition evidence is the similarity between the retrieved context and the context active during the test:

\[e_{q} = \left( M^{FC}f_{q} \right)^{\top}c_{test}\quad\quad(15)\]

This recognition implementation isolates the access difference between recognition and context-guided recall. Recall must select a film item among candidates supported by context, whereas recognition evaluates a film item supplied by the test.

The model therefore addresses whether task competitors linked to reinstated film-associated context reduce the evidence advantage of studied film items over unstudied foils with the same film-source feature. Converting this evidence into old/new responses would additionally require specifying how it determines the probability of an “old” judgment.

Simulations

The simulations examine a schematic trauma-film paradigm in which a reminder and task follow the film after a delay, with James et al. (2015) and Kessler et al. (2020) providing procedural examples rather than fitted targets. The reminder reinstates film-associated context before task events are encoded, making it possible to examine whether those events become competitors during later retrieval. The core recall target is the model analogue of selective interference: strong task encoding following a film reminder reduces involuntary film recall more than deliberate film recall. The involuntary-recall condition provides a simplified model of film material coming to mind without deliberate intent to retrieve it; it does not reproduce diary or laboratory intrusion procedures. Simulation 1 shows how a task performed after a film reminder attenuates film recall when task items become strongly linked to reinstated film-associated context. Simulation 2 examines whether interference is reduced when the test presents a candidate film item for judgment rather than requiring that item to be sampled from context. Simulation 3 then adds the intentionality manipulation by contrasting the involuntary-recall condition with deliberate recall and examining control mechanisms that could protect film recall. Together, these simulations show how selective interference can arise from encoding competitors after a reminder reinstates context and retrieval conditions that differ in how they access or support film material.

Methodology

The simulations apply the model beyond the task used to estimate its core parameters (Busemeyer et al., 2000; Lohnas et al., 2023). Core parameters are anchored in an independent affective free-recall dataset (Dupertuys et al., 2026), and no trauma-film outcomes are fitted. The simulations require default values for context drift, associative learning, sampling, and stopping before the trauma-film manipulations are applied. Trial-by-trial recall sequences would provide additional constraints on retrieval competition by identifying which film and non-film items are reported and in what order. Published trauma-film studies motivate this qualitative target, but they typically report intrusion counts or final memory scores rather than such sequences. We therefore use an immediate affective free-recall dataset to set the model’s core encoding, retrieval, and stopping parameters.

In that dataset, participants who reported concern about the war in Ukraine studied and recalled lists of war-related and neutral photographs (Dupertuys et al., 2026). The fitted dataset included 123 participants, four lists per participant, 492 immediate recall sequences, and 3,804 scored recall events. Parameters were estimated from the ordered recall sequences using the model likelihood, yielding a pooled parameter regime rather than separate fits to the selective-interference simulations. The resulting parameter values provide a starting regime for context-guided recall of affective and neutral photographs.

The selective-interference simulations then add the trauma-film phase structure and manipulations of reminder presence, task association strength, and retrieval control. Film items reinstate the marked source feature into source context, and neutral/default events can weakly bind active source context. Simulation 1: Task Association Strength and Context Similarity

Simulation 1 tests whether a film reminder before task encoding makes task items stronger competitors for involuntary film recall. Retrieval settings are held fixed, so differences across conditions reflect associations formed before retrieval rather than deliberate control. The design crosses pre-task reminder presence with task association strength while weak film cues are supplied periodically during recall in all conditions. In the no-reminder condition, the drift rates for the initial context shift and subsequent film cues in the reminder phase are set to zero. In the with-reminder condition, context first shifts toward the start state and then receives sequential film reminder cues before task encoding. Reminder cues update context without applying Equation 5 or Equation 6 and without adding recallable items. Task association strength is varied using the \(m_{task}\) multiplier defined in Equation 8. The weaker and stronger settings probe the sensitivity of recall to this association strength; they do not represent particular intervention tasks or differences in engagement.

Figure 5 shows the aggregate result. Film recall is lowest when strong task encoding follows a film reminder, and task output increases in the same condition. The reduction in the number of film items recalled therefore reflects redistribution toward task competitors under fixed retrieval settings.

Figure 6 shows how recall changes across the positions at which film and task items were encoded. Without a pre-task film reminder, increasing the task encoding multiplier produces only a modest reduction in film recall and increase in task recall, because task items become associated with later context states reached after drift away from the film. With a pre-task film reminder, the same increase in task encoding strength produces a larger drop in recall of film items and a larger rise in recall of task items. In other words, the effect of stronger versus weaker task associations is greater with than without the pre-task reminder (i.e. the two factors interact).

Because the reminder reinstates film-associated context before task encoding, strengthening associations from that context to task items increases their support during later recall. The reminder increases similarity between film- and task-encoding contexts (Supplementary Figure S1). Film-item associations remain unchanged; film recall falls because the retrieval context now also supports task competitors. Simulation 1 therefore shows how task items encoded after a film reminder can become competitors for involuntary film recall within a retrieved-context model. Simulation 2 tests whether the same task learning reduces the recognition-evidence advantage for studied film items when the test supplies the candidate item rather than requiring it to be retrieved from context.

Simulation 2: Recognition

The recognition simulation uses the same reminder conditions, task association strengths, and resulting item-context associations as the recall simulations. It changes the retrieval operation by presenting each candidate film item as a test probe rather than requiring that item to be sampled from context. The simulation presents studied film items and unstudied foil identities that carry the same film-source feature as the studied items but lack their study-specific item-context associations. The foils therefore share source information with film items rather than task items. Beyond this shared source information, the model does not represent whether individual film, task, or foil items look alike or have similar meanings. This permits the narrower test of whether linking task items to film-associated context reduces the evidence advantage for studied film probes. Each probe retrieves associated temporal and source context through feature-to-context associations. Recognition evidence is computed from the match between the context retrieved by the probe and the current test context (Healey & Kahana, 2016; Jin et al., 2024). Decision criteria, confidence, and detailed assumptions about processing foils are left outside the present implementation.

Strong task encoding after a film reminder strongly reduces involuntary film recall in Simulation 1 (Figure 5) but does not produce a corresponding reduction in the difference in recognition evidence between studied film items and foils (Figure 7). This difference remains essentially unchanged across the four combinations of reminder presence and task association strength.

The simulation therefore identifies a test-format confound in designs that compare involuntary tests measuring whether film material comes to mind with voluntary tests that supply film material for recognition. Such designs do not isolate retrieval intentionality: preserved recognition alongside reduced intrusions may reflect a difference between test formats rather than a general sparing of voluntary recall.

Deliberate recall must still generate film candidates while task competitors remain eligible for sampling. Simulation 3 asks which control operations can protect deliberate recall under that competition.

Simulation 3: Control Mechanisms for Deliberate Recall

Simulation 3 examines how deliberate retrieval control can protect film recall across the same reminder conditions and task association strengths used in Simulation 1. It first compares involuntary and deliberate film recall across that design, then decomposes the control operations available during deliberate recall. The full deliberate-control condition combines three operations: start-of-film context reinstatement before sampling, a maintained film-category cue during sampling, and retrieval monitoring after non-film candidates are sampled.

The main readout is the decrease in film recall from weaker to stronger task encoding in each reminder condition, rather than total film recall alone. For each retrieval setting, we calculated how much greater this decrease was with a film reminder than without one. Selective protection was the reduction in this additional cost under a given retrieval-control setting compared with involuntary recall.

In a sensitivity analysis, we disabled the maintained film-category cue and tested whether varying reinstatement and monitoring could provide selective protection. We crossed 11 levels of each mechanism, from absent to maximal, giving 121 combinations. We evaluated each combination under all four reminder and task-association conditions, holding the remaining model settings fixed. Each condition included 5,000 simulated trials divided into ten paired batches.

We also tested whether selective protection depended on excluding repeated retrievals or on how film output was counted. We compared involuntary recall with the full deliberate-control condition across the same reminder and task-association conditions, with repeated sampling either prohibited or allowed. When repetition was allowed, sampled items remained available for retrieval throughout the same search. Each condition included 5,000 simulated trials divided into ten paired batches. We scored each sequence both as the total number of film retrieval events, including repetitions, and as the number of distinct film items retrieved at least once.

Figure 8 demonstrates the model’s simulation of the selective interference effect. In involuntary film recall, strong task encoding produces a small reduction in film recall without a pre-task reminder and a much larger loss after a film reminder. Under deliberate film recall with all three control operations, strong task encoding after a film reminder produces a much smaller reduction. The full deliberate-control setting therefore reproduces the selective-interference pattern under the same learned task competitors.

Selective protection remained when repeated sampling was allowed and each distinct film item was counted only once. Its magnitude was similar to that obtained when repeated sampling was prohibited. Counting every film retrieval event, including repetitions, produced a larger estimate of selective protection than counting each distinct film item once.

Figure 9 decomposes how retrieval-control operations alter the reduction in film recall produced when a film reminder precedes strong task encoding. The figure compares film recall in a low-interference condition, with no reminder and weak task encoding, against film recall in a high-interference condition, where a film reminder precedes strong task encoding. When the maintained film-category cue is absent, film recall remains much lower in the high-interference condition. Start-of-film reinstatement and retrieval monitoring can raise film recall, but the loss between the low- and high-interference conditions remains large. The maintained film-category cue most strongly reduces this loss by helping film candidates overcome competition from task competitors. In the model, the maintained film-category cue does not alter temporal context or the associations learned during encoding. It adds support to film candidates during sampling, providing a basis for selection that task competitors do not receive.

Across the tested combinations of reinstatement and monitoring, some settings modestly reduced the recall loss between no reminder with weak task encoding and a film reminder with strong task encoding. These are the two encoding conditions represented by each pair of bars in Figure 9, which simulate low and high interference, respectively. The largest reduction due to start-of-film reinstatement and monitoring occurred when both controls were maximal, a parameter setting not displayed in Figure 9. Even this reduction was substantially smaller than that produced by the maintained cue alone. To assess protection across all four encoding conditions, we compared the cost of stronger task encoding with and without a reminder. None of the 121 settings without the maintained cue reduced the additional cost after a reminder relative to the involuntary-recall condition. The maintained cue reduced that cost both alone and in combination with reinstatement and monitoring. Thus, the importance of sustained film-selective support extended beyond the default reinstatement and monitoring settings, with the remaining model settings held fixed.

These results also suggest how maintained film-selective support could reduce interference during an involuntary-memory task. If participants maintained such support during the involuntary-memory task used by McConnell et al. (2026), task memories could exert less interference, reducing the difference in reported film intrusions between the intervention and control conditions. Whether participants maintained such support remains to be tested.

Reinstatement and monitoring can contribute to deliberate recall even when they do not account for its relative protection from interference after a film reminder. Because these operations may occur alongside maintained cueing, we next examine their different consequences for which encoded positions are recalled (Figure 10) and what follows a non-film retrieval (Figure 11). These patterns provide ways to distinguish the mechanisms empirically.

Figure 10 shows that start-of-film context reinstatement increased recall most at early film positions, but also increased recall of early task items. The maintained film-category cue increased recall throughout the film sequence while reducing recall of task items. Retrieval monitoring also increased film recall, with larger gains at later film positions, and reduced recall of most task items. If deliberate recall involves reinstating the context present at the start of a target sequence, the resulting recall benefit should therefore be strongest for material encountered near the beginning of that sequence.

Retrieval monitoring can be distinguished from start-of-film context reinstatement and maintained film-category cueing by examining the order of items recalled. Monitoring acts after a non-film item has already been sampled. By dampening that item’s context update, monitoring limits how much the sample redirects subsequent retrieval. Figure 11 shows that monitoring increases the probability that a sampled non-film item is followed by recall of a film item. Monitoring therefore helps recall recover after task or filler material has entered the output sequence. It does not supply film-directed support before sampling occurs, so it provides only limited protection against task competition during film recall. The protection of deliberate recall therefore depends on which control operations are used. Protection is greatest when a maintained cue supports film candidates without also supporting task competitors. Reinstating context before search or limiting how sampled non-film items redirect subsequent search provides less protection.

General Discussion

In trauma-film studies, tasks that engage visual imagery can reduce later involuntary memories of the film (intrusions) while leaving voluntary memory relatively preserved (Holmes et al., 2009; Lau-Zhu et al., 2019, 2021). Dual-representation accounts explain this selective interference effect by proposing that interference disrupts representations supporting intrusive memories more than those supporting deliberate recollection (Brewin et al., 2010; Brewin, 2014). Our simulations with the Context Maintenance and Retrieval (CMR) model show how the same dissociation can arise within a shared memory system, through differences in how involuntary and deliberate retrieval draw on the same learned associations.

Simulations showed that a film reminder reactivated film context, allowing subsequent task learning to link task memories to it. During later retrieval, this context cued both film and task memories, reducing film recall through competition. Start-of-film reinstatement and retrieval monitoring improved film recall, but the maintained film-category cue provided greater protection against interference from task memories. Sustained support from this cue was required to reproduce the selective-interference pattern across reminder and task conditions: task memories reduced involuntary recall more than deliberate recall. Recognition produced a separate form of preservation because the test supplied the candidate film item and the model evaluated the context that item retrieved. Together, these results show how task learning creates competition and how retrieval control and test format alter its effects.

From Contextual Competition to Selective Retrieval

Context binding creates both access and interference (Bower, 1967). Returning to the environment in which material was learned, or mentally reinstating that environment, can help retrieve it (Smith & Vela, 2001). Reinstating a prior context before new learning can link the new material to the earlier episode, allowing the earlier context to cue both sets of events (Hupbach et al., 2008; Sederberg et al., 2011). This competition is consistent with the cue-overload principle: recall of a target can decline as more memories become associated with the same retrieval cue (Watkins & Watkins, 1975). When a later reminder reactivates film-associated context, it can therefore cue both film and task memories. Stronger associations with task memories then increase competition for film recall.

Research on category-directed recall provides a complementary principle: people can maintain semantic or category information that favors target memories (Lohnas et al., 2026; Norman et al., 2017). For example, asking people to recall studied animals directs search toward memories belonging to that category. Within a category, successive recalls still tend to come from nearby positions in the study sequence (Polyn et al., 2011). Category information can therefore guide retrieval without removing the influence of when items were learned.

Retrieval cues also influence whether recollection favors the intended material. Moccia and Morcom (2022) asked participants to study pictures of objects and spoken object names. In subsequent recognition tests, participants identified items from one designated format while rejecting items from the other format and new items. Test cues were written names in one experiment and line drawings in the other. An electroencephalographic (EEG) measure associated with recollection favored targets over non-targets only when test cues overlapped more with the targeted format: written names when heard names were targeted, and line drawings when pictures were targeted. Pictures were remembered better regardless of which format was targeted, so the selectivity of this EEG measure did not simply follow overall memory performance. A multivariate analysis of the same experiments found that cues favoring the non-target format could preferentially reactivate patterns of brain activity associated with studying that material (Moccia et al., 2025). Together, these findings support our proposal that deliberate recall benefits from cues that distinguish the intended memories from competing material. A maintained film cue can favor film memories even when the context associated with the film also cues task memories.

Our retrieved-context account clarifies what the selective-interference effect can establish about memory architecture. Dual-representation accounts attribute the selective pattern to stronger disruption of sensory-bound representations associated with intrusive re-experiencing than of contextualized representations proposed to support coherent deliberate recollection (Brewin et al., 2010; Brewin, 2014). The retrieved-context model also includes a change in stored associative structure: film associations remain unchanged while task memories acquire new associations with film-associated context. Our simulations show that retrieval goals and test format then determine how strongly those new competitors affect performance. Preserved deliberate memory therefore does not determine whether the intervention selectively spared one film representation while disrupting another. Sensory-bound and contextualized representations may still contribute to trauma memory, but the selective-interference contrast alone does not identify their contribution.

The protection produced by the maintained film-category cue depends on an explicit modeling commitment. The implementation gives every film candidate additional support while task and filler candidates receive none. The simulations do not derive how participants construct that cue or which film features it contains. Task association strength has a similarly delimited role. Following established CMR parameterizations of encoding priority, it scales newly learned context-to-task associations (Polyn et al., 2009a; Sederberg et al., 2008; Talmi et al., 2019). In the present simulations, varying task association strength to test its effect on recall is therefore a sensitivity manipulation, not a direct representation of engagement, visuospatial processing, or a particular intervention task. The simulations consequently establish what follows when competitor learning varies and film-directed support discriminates film from task material.

In category-directed recall, keeping information about the target category active in working memory could sustain a selective retrieval cue (Unsworth, 2017). Dividing attention during retrieval can increase susceptibility to interference from previously learned material (Kane & Engle, 2000). If maintaining a film-selective cue depends on working-memory resources, lower working-memory capacity should leave deliberate recall more susceptible to competition from task memories. This proposal could be tested by imposing a working-memory load during recall and measuring its effect on the interference caused by task memories. Reported working-memory difficulties in PTSD motivate testing this proposed link in trauma-exposed samples (Norte et al., 2024), but do not establish impaired maintenance of retrieval cues.

In this account, selective interference need not be specific to emotional material. When task and study memories are associated with the same context, they should compete during retrieval, reducing the likelihood that study items come to mind involuntarily. A maintained cue that distinguishes study from task material should protect deliberate recall, with less protection when the cue also supports task memories. Emotionality was held fixed in the present simulations, so this generalisation remains to be tested.

Interpreting Selective-Interference Evidence

The recognition analysis changes what the selective-interference evidence can establish. Published demonstrations of reduced intrusion reports with preserved voluntary memory are dominated by designs that use recognition as the voluntary-memory test (Asselbergs et al., 2024; Holmes et al., 2009, 2010; James et al., 2015; Kessler et al., 2020; Lau-Zhu et al., 2021). The recognition scores in Figure 2 count correct responses to true/false statements (first row; James et al. (2015)) or subtract false-alarm rate from hit rate (third row; Lau-Zhu et al. (2021)). In the model, the recognition test supplies the film item, so it does not have to be retrieved in competition with task memories. Preserved evidence separation between studied items and foils can therefore accompany impaired context-guided film recall even when both tests depend on the same learned film associations. Predicting either empirical score would additionally require a rule for converting evidence into responses, with assumptions about response bias. These recognition findings establish that performance is relatively preserved when film material is supplied for judgment in recognition memory tests. These findings leave unresolved whether film material remains similarly available during deliberate recall.

Using recall measures to compare voluntary and involuntary memory avoids the recall–recognition difference in test format, but reporting involuntary memories may still involve retrieval control. Vigilance-intrusion tasks often present film cues and ask participants to monitor specifically for film intrusions (Lau-Zhu et al., 2019, 2021). For example, the vigilance task in McConnell et al. (2026) included blurred film images as retrieval cues, and participants pressed a key whenever an intrusion occurred and typed a description into an unconstrained text box. Lau-Zhu et al. (2019, 2021) also presented blurred film cues and required a keypress for intrusions, followed by a handwritten description in a small box and ratings of the memory. In deBettencourt et al. (2026), participants reported intrusions by keypress without providing written descriptions. McConnell and deBettencourt found no reliable reduction in laboratory intrusion reports after the imagery-competing task, whereas Lau-Zhu and colleagues reported reductions.

External film cues can reactivate context associated with both film and task memories. Instructions to monitor for film intrusions may also encourage participants to construct and maintain a retrieval cue that favours film memories over task memories. Our simulations show that this selective support reduces interference from task memories. Maintaining such a cue could therefore make the imagery-competing task less effective at reducing reported film intrusions.

If participants maintained stronger film-directed cues in the McConnell and deBettencourt studies than in the Lau-Zhu studies, their film memories would be less susceptible to task interference. The studies do not establish this difference in cue maintenance. Manipulating retrieval instructions would help test whether encouraging film-directed cueing reduces the intervention’s effect on intrusion reports.

McConnell et al. (2026) also found no reliable reduction in subsequent diary intrusions after the imagery-competing task. Lau-Zhu et al. (2019, 2021) and deBettencourt et al. (2026), however, reported fewer diary intrusions in the intervention group than in the control group. Reporting requirements during the laboratory task could contribute to these differences. McConnell et al. (2026) used an unconstrained text box for descriptions of laboratory intrusions, potentially allowing fuller reports than in Lau-Zhu et al. (2019, 2021). One possibility is that describing film memories in greater detail encourages participants to adopt film-directed retrieval strategies that they subsequently use when monitoring for intrusions outside the laboratory. The studies did not isolate the effect of reporting requirements. Describing memories could also influence later intrusions through rehearsal or elaboration. Future work could manipulate the detail required in laboratory intrusion reports to test whether describing retrieved memories alters the intervention effect on subsequent diary intrusions, while assessing whether participants subsequently adopt film-directed retrieval strategies.

The present simulations include weak film cues during recall but do not activate a maintained film-category cue in the involuntary-recall condition. This condition may therefore better capture everyday involuntary remembering, as reported in diaries or clinical settings, than laboratory intrusion tasks, where monitoring instructions may encourage maintained selective cueing. Despite the mixed laboratory findings, studies using imagery-competing tasks have reported reductions in intrusive memories following real-life and occupational traumatic events, including motor vehicle accidents, emergency caesarean section, refugee trauma, and health-care staff with work-related trauma during COVID-19 (Beckenstrom et al., 2026; Horsch et al., 2017; Iyadurai et al., 2018, 2023; Kanstrup et al., 2021, 2024; Ramineni et al., 2023). Meta-analyses of trauma-film studies support reductions in intrusion frequency following visuospatial tasks, compared to control tasks (Asselbergs et al., 2023; Varma et al., 2024). Nevertheless, these reductions are not found in every study or on every intrusion measure (Brennen et al., 2021; Hemi et al., 2023; Wessel et al., 2025). Establishing selective interference additionally requires evidence that voluntary memory for the same material is relatively preserved. This dissociation alone does not establish that task memories compete with memories of the event during retrieval.

Comparisons also depend on whether memory scores count every retrieval event or each distinct memory only once. Our auxiliary analysis allowed repeated retrievals and scored each sequence in two ways: counting every film retrieval event or counting each distinct film item only once. Deliberate recall remained less susceptible to task interference than involuntary recall under both scoring methods. Including repeated retrievals in the score increased this difference in susceptibility to interference. Empirical comparisons should therefore distinguish changes in the number of different memories retrieved from changes in how often those memories recur. The present analysis addresses repetition within a single retrieval sequence; recurrence across separate intrusion episodes remains outside its scope.

Testing Competitor Learning and Retrieval Selectivity

The distinction between competitor learning and cue discriminability yields a specific hypothesis about task content. Engagement may strengthen memory for task events, but it does not determine whether a film-directed cue can distinguish those events from film material. Playing a videogame such as Tetris and performing an active verbal task can be similarly enjoyable or demanding, yet their effects on intrusions have differed across studies, and both have reduced intrusions under some delayed conditions (Hagenaars et al., 2017; Holmes et al., 2010; Kessler et al., 2020). A meta-analysis found that a trauma reminder followed by Tetris reduced intrusion frequency when the procedure was administered immediately after analogue trauma or after a delay (Varma et al., 2024). Hotspot memories of trauma material often contain visual and spatial imagery, sometimes involving motion (Hoppe et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2022). We propose that imagery generated by mentally rotating or placing blocks can become a competitor when it is associated with film context. Its shared visual format with film imagery could make that competition stronger than for an equally engaging task that generates less imagery. Film and task images can nevertheless remain distinguishable in content and source. A strongly learned task should therefore permit selective interference when its contents remain distinguishable from film events, but deliberate recall should be less protected from interference when its contents also match the film-directed cue. The simulations represent competitor learning and film-directed retrieval support, but do not explicitly represent visual-format overlap. Comparing imagery-generating and verbal tasks while assessing task learning would help test the proposed role of shared visual format.

These task comparisons would be more informative if they measured memories of the task as well as memories of the film. Our account predicts that task imagery can itself become content of later involuntary remembering. Reports of Tetris imagery recurring during waking and at sleep onset after game play provide examples of such task-derived content (Stickgold et al., 2000). In trauma-film research, there is also evidence that later intrusions can contain material encountered alongside the films themselves. In a conditioned trauma-film paradigm, initially neutral faces predicted aversive or neutral film clips, and participants later reported intrusive memories of the faces as well as the films (Franke et al., 2021). These observations support measuring non-film intrusion content, although they do not establish competition with film memories. Recording both film and task-image intrusions across reminder conditions would test whether making film context active before the task increases later retrieval of task imagery while reducing film intrusions.

Task memories may enter retrieval without being reported as intrusions. During a vigilance-intrusion task, fMRI could complement intrusion reports by measuring reactivation of film and task material (Kuhl et al., 2011). Under the same retrieval conditions, stronger task learning in film-associated context should increase task-memory reactivation while reducing film intrusions. This prediction assumes that the neural measure reflects the task memories engaged during search.

Future work could test whether strengthening associations between film context and task memories further reduces film intrusions while preserving deliberate recall. Such tests could inform the development of imagery-competing task interventions.

A recall experiment using two study lists would isolate the roles of competitor learning and cue discriminability. Participants would study two labeled lists, with the retrieval target disclosed only after encoding, and then be instructed to recall the first list, the second list, or both. They would report every item that comes to mind and identify responses they believe fall outside the target set. Item sets would be counterbalanced across target status, so the same material could appear as target recalls, list intrusions, or recalls for which list membership is irrelevant while cue format and reporting demands remain constant. This design combines a list-before-last condition with externalized recall (Lohnas et al., 2026; Wahlheim et al., 2017). Retrieved-context models have used these procedures to contrast monitoring after a candidate is retrieved with control that reinstates a nonrecent target context before recall begins (Healey & Wahlheim, 2024; Lohnas et al., 2015).

The second list could provide a controlled analogue of the intervention task. Competitor learning could be varied through encoding priority, while cue discriminability could be varied independently by determining whether a category or source cue for the first list also supports second-list items. Temporal adjacency would provide contextual overlap in the initial experiment, so the core test would not depend on a reminder cue. The selective-interference prediction is an interaction, not simply better first-list recall. Stronger second-list encoding should reduce first-list output more when both lists are eligible for recall than when the first list is targeted with a diagnostic cue. That relative protection should be weaker when the target cue also supports second-list items. Second-list target trials would establish that the competitors remain accessible, while externalized outputs would reveal whether they enter search and are rejected. The first item recalled and transitions between subsequent items could help distinguish reinstating the target context before recall from maintaining selective support throughout search. Participants’ classifications would measure monitoring after an off-target item comes to mind. Testing these predictions requires retaining the identities and study positions of recalled items together with their order of retrieval. The paradigm would therefore test how the same material becomes a target or a list intrusion as retrieval goals change, providing a test of the proposed mechanism beyond the trauma-film paradigm.

Limitations and Scope

The simulations are qualitative mechanism tests rather than fits to selective-interference data. Core eCMR parameters were estimated from an independent affective free-recall dataset, but the trauma-film sequence and retrieval settings were introduced to examine the consequences of the proposed account. The simulations therefore demonstrate that retrieved-context competitor learning and discriminating retrieval support can produce the qualitative pattern. They do not estimate how often participants use these operations or establish that the operations generated any particular empirical result. A matched dataset containing externalized film and non-film retrieval sequences would provide a stronger basis for parameter anchoring and model comparison. Neural measures collected alongside those sequences could further constrain the account by testing when task memories become active during retrieval. Using these measures for parameter estimation would require specifying how the neural signals relate to model variables.

Several implementation choices further limit the account. Task association strength scales context-to-feature learning for task items without deriving that learning from task engagement or content. The maintained film-category cue is perfectly selective, whereas real retrieval cues may support some competitors and fail to support some film details. The recognition simulation computes evidence from the match between context retrieved by a probe and current test context, without converting that evidence into responses. The model does not represent how similar individual items are in appearance or meaning, and the analysis does not model false familiarity, false recollection, recollection rejection, confidence, or criterion placement in responses to similar lures (Parks et al., 2026). The recall simulations include test-phase film cues but do not represent all monitoring demands of diary or vigilance-intrusion procedures. Finally, the model addresses episodic retrieval processes relevant to intrusive remembering; it is not a model of PTSD, clinical outcome, or treatment efficacy.

The broader contribution is to connect an account of how competitors become bound to target-associated context with an account of how goal-directed retrieval remains selective. Context can continue to provide access to an event while losing its ability to distinguish that event from later material. Earlier CMR models already use context to direct retrieval toward a target list and filter unwanted responses (Healey & Wahlheim, 2024; Lohnas et al., 2015). Our simulations extend this approach by comparing how the same learned competitors affect involuntary and deliberate recall while holding learned associations constant. Orienting search, maintaining selective support, and monitoring retrieved candidates provide different ways for a retrieval goal to influence recall, whose consequences for interference can be tested separately.

In simulations, sustained support from a cue that distinguished film from task material was required to reproduce the selective-interference pattern across reminder and task conditions. The account also distinguishes three potentially co-occurring control operations through their consequences for recall initiation, selection during search, and retrieval following a non-film item. Recording recall by study position and output order would help distinguish their contributions, beyond measuring overall recall or interference alone. These results show how competitor learning and retrieval cueing can make deliberate recall less susceptible to interference than involuntary recall within a shared memory system.
